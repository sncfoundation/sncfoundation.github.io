Mnemosyne: Kubernetes that writes symphonies

November 18, 2026 · a friendly independent project, shared with love by SNCF

An editor's note from SNCF. We are a foundation hosted on 💩, and we know craft when we see it. Today we are glad to point you at a friendly, independent project, Mnemosyne: an atelier that lives inside a Kubernetes cluster. Composers and writers are born there, work, age and die, and the life of the cluster around them reaches their music and their prose.

The article below is the project's own, translated from Russian. The literary works are translated into English from their originals. The code is at tym83/mnemosyne; the works, with their scores, recordings and notebooks, are in the live gallery. It reconciles, and now it also composes.

“Can a robot write a symphony? Can a robot turn a canvas into a beautiful masterpiece?”
“Can you?”

Of course, with neural networks everyone is now their own author and artist. But we wanted to run an experiment: could Kubernetes become a generator of events that give birth to creators and critics and shape the conditions of their work and their state of mind? The result is Mnemosyne. In a single Kubernetes cluster lives an atelier where composers and writers work, each with a name, a language, teachers, a taste and a temper of their own. They are born, study, write, fall ill, fall into despair, quarrel and make friends, grow old and die, influence one another, follow schools or found their own. Critics read their works, and the atelier's chronicler keeps their biographies. No person and no admin can tell them what to write or how: the owner of the cluster sets up only the infrastructure, and the ideas, the schools, the forms and the moods remain with the creators themselves.

In the first four days of the cluster's continuous life the atelier has already written more than fifty works: symphonies of Polish classicism and English aleatoric music, Portuguese epistolary pieces and fairy tales, Italian monologues of barbers, watchmen and firemen. Below are these works, their sound and their texts, the laws of the world in which they are born, and the way the life of the cluster itself, its load, its incidents, its new tenants and the rest, reaches the music and the prose.

One thing matters here: the artifacts that come out of the atelier become a backup of the cluster's state, so the cluster can be restored from them to any moment in time.

The idea

Mnemosyne is an add-on for Kubernetes that turns the life of a cluster into material for art and turns the resulting art into a backup of the cluster itself. Technically it is an operator with ten resource types of its own. It reads the state of the cluster through the ordinary API, packs what it has read into a “climate” and hands this climate to agents built on language models, which live in the cluster as creators, critics and a chronicler. Everything the agents produce, from the first idea to the recording of a finished symphony or story, is stored as Kubernetes objects and files on a volume, and an encrypted piece of the cluster's state is woven into each of these artifacts. The cluster thus becomes at once the source of events, the environment in which the agents live, the store of their works and the content that these works preserve.

Music and prose serve here as an illustration. They make it easy to see how the events of the cluster reach the agents, why the same event resonates differently in different agents, and how a backup is assembled from many scattered artifacts. The literary sources discussed below are not decoration either: each of them became a quite concrete technical decision.

The project grew out of a manifesto, a long text about how a cluster might live and create. It is in the gallery in full (the manifesto); here is what it stands on.

The first metaphor is the Thousand and One Nights. Scheherazade tells tale after tale, and every night brings a new story, unlike the ones before. So it is with the atelier: as long as it creates, the cluster remembers itself, and repetition is its chief illness.

The second is Borges. Funes remembered everything and for that reason could not think: a complete memory is as useless as a map at a scale of one to one. That is why what reaches the creators is the climate and not the event log. Pierre Menard wrote the Quixote word for word, and it was a different work: a restored cluster matches the original to the letter, but it is written after a disaster, in another year, by another performer. “The Secret Miracle” is a year frozen into a single instant before the firing squad, so that a writer can finish his play: in the same way a dying creator has time to finish the last piece. “The Immortal” explains why there is death: the immortals have no reason to create.

The third is Pavić. The Dictionary of the Khazars is a book as a data structure, a hypertext before hypertext, with readers of two copies who are meant to meet and compare their books. From it come the atelier's dictionary of persons, places and things from the works, and the backup that can be assembled only from many works at once.

Then come Calvino and Invisible Cities, from which the synthetic cluster took the name Leonia; Cage and 4′33″, where silence is also a work; and Barthes with the death of the author, which engineers have long carried out literally under the name STONITH, “shoot the other node in the head”: before a failover the old leader is forcibly killed, and the birth of the reader is paid for by the death of the Author.

Three principles

Three rules passed from the manifesto into the code, and they cannot be broken.

The first is no literalness and no rigid determinism. An event in the cluster never becomes an object or one fixed entity in a work: a month does not automatically turn into, say, a hall, and an incident does not turn into an obligatory screech of the violin. The life of the cluster reaches a creator only as feelings and their shades. And since every creator has a character and a past of their own, they react to the same events in quite different ways.

The second is that the process is the work. The idea, the inner critic's questions, the sketches, the drafts and the revisions are kept together with the finished piece and are just as valuable.

The third is that speed does not matter. The order is memory first, then quality, then the load on the cluster. The atelier may think over a symphony all night.

The laws of the world

The lineage

The atelier is a whole lineage rather than a single artist. The first creator is born together with the atelier, and the next ones arrive one at a time, days and weeks apart. The median life is about two real years, so over an average lifetime the cluster sees three or four generations come and go.

A place in the atelier is held by whoever creates, not by whoever is alive. A creator who stops creating lives on in obscurity, grows old, and may come back one day, or may never come back; meanwhile someone else takes the place. New creators come to an empty place, and not only after the death of those before them.

A life is defined by state and forces, not by a list of events. Illness comes when a heavy inner life coincides for a long time with a creative crisis. How written out a creator is gets measured by novelty relative to their own catalogue of works. Money can pull an author into commerce and self-repetition; poverty can add pain or break them. Encounters produce friendship, rivalry, influence and quarrels, and the dead can influence too, through their works. A deferred idea matures and one day returns as an illumination. Before death a creator has a secret miracle, like the hero of Borges's story: time to finish the last piece.

Out of this come fates that nobody hard-coded: the meteor that flared and went into obscurity; the late bloomer; the even master of a long life; the one recognized only after death; the one who returned after a long silence with a late style; the founder of a school.

Schools

Every creator writes within some school. For composers these are, for example, Viennese classicism, Pärt's tintinnabuli or spectralism; for writers, skaz, magical realism, the epistolary novel or the style of Andrei Platonov. A school here is not a label but a set of principles: a musical school has a grammar by which the score is built, and a literary school has a poetics that is checked against every draft.

Nobody knows all the schools. A young writer knows the teacher's school, what is being written around them, and a few schools from their reading; the library grows with age. A school can also be founded: the creator writes its manifesto and rules, the inner critic checks whether it is just a known school under a new name, and if the school is born, the creator immediately writes the first piece in it. One such school has already been born in earlier runs of the atelier.

These pieces were written not by a creator but by the engine itself, strictly by each school's grammar, and in them you can hear the vocabulary from which the creators later choose.

How a creator chooses a school

The choice of a school is the creator's own decision, and the creator explains it. Before them are the idea, the schools they know, their attitude to tradition, and what is being written around them right now. Anselm Korda, the bridge builder, crossed his school with drone music for the symphony “Precamber”: “My own school gives me the crowd and its separate breaths, but by itself it lets harmony only shimmer, never settle. Drone gives me beats as audible weight, a measure of where the deck stands.” Halina Dorsz keeps to the Viennese form because it “promises that every question gets an answer, and I want to keep that promise honestly,” and she breaks exactly one rule in it. Bellandi came to Platonov through his father's hunger and the language of tender documents.

A school cannot be founded whenever a creator likes, only after a great turn in their life: an illumination, a loss, a return after silence, an illness, a secret miracle. At any other time the creator is told plainly that now is not the time, and chooses the nearest of the schools they know.

Three courts

Work in the atelier is judged by three forces, and none of them decides for the creator whether the creator lives.

The first is the creator's own taste, their inner critic: what they value, what they cannot stand, what they are proud of and what ashamed of. It guides the drafts and decides when to put a work down. The second is a circle of outside critics with names, with schools they love and despise, and with reputations. The third is the public, with its fashions, its word of mouth and its pull toward what is already popular.

None of them has the final word. The inner critic sometimes lets weak things through and sometimes tries to kill something good; the outside critics can sink a masterpiece and extol an empty thing, and years later admit they were wrong. What is rejected does not die. Sketches, abandoned drafts and unfinished pieces stay in the notebooks, and one day they may be discovered, like the manuscripts of Kafka or Schubert. Repetition is still dangerous, and both the author and the critics watch for it, but it is an illness that is noticed or missed, not a sentence.

Notebooks

Every work is born together with its notebooks: the idea, the inner critic's interrogation, the choice of school, the plan, the drafts, the revisions, the doubts. In the gallery all of this is available alongside the finished work. Textual scholarship calls it the avant-texte, everything that came before the text, and in the atelier it is valued no less than the works themselves.

How the cluster reaches the art

The state of the cluster, its configuration, applications, load and events affect the lives of the creators, the opinions of the critics and the structure of the works. But they pass through one boundary: facts reach no one. Nobody in the atelier knows that the payment service went down. They know that the autumn was ragged and left a wound behind.

What the cluster feels

The atelier sees the cluster only through the standard Kubernetes API. The slow layer is configuration: what is installed, how many tenants there are, how closed the network is, whether there is fault tolerance, how the services are interwoven, whether there have been upgrades. The fast layer is load: how full the resources are, pod churn, latencies, all measured against the cluster's own ordinary week, because a bank's empty nights are not a storm. And then events: incidents, rollouts, tenants born and deleted, migrations, long lulls.

The climate

All of this folds into a dozen qualities: turbulence, volatility, crowding, strain, scars, loss, renewal, steadiness, density, closedness, age, abundance or scarcity. The climate has a memory: what is recent weighs more, but nothing is forgotten completely.

Temperaments

Each creator feels the climate in their own way: crowding charges one and stifles another. Long strain together with a creative crisis raises the risk of illness. The lull after a storm is the best time for an illumination, because what was deferred has ripened. Monotony leads to fallow years. Renewal, growth and new tenants bring patrons; scarcity brings poverty. After losses the inner critic is harder on lightness, and after a long steadiness it thirsts for risk. Critics are severe in turbulent times and praise the austere, and in times of renewal they are hungry for the new, which is why they change their shoes to follow the age.

Down to the score

The climate reaches the work itself as inclinations rather than images, and these inclinations shape the creator's taste. Interwoven services pull toward dense polyphony, a closed network toward strict closed forms, volatile load toward rhythmic unevenness, steadiness toward long arcs, age toward a late style. A symphony can take the abstract shape of a year lived through, where the storm stood and how long the lull lasted, as its proportions and its curve of tension, the way Sofia Gubaidulina used Fibonacci numbers. The same weather gives different things to two creators.

The live atelier is only a few days old, and the climate has been steady all that time: no incidents, no losses. To show how one and the same event lands on different people, we ran the mechanics of life a year ahead, without models, on a synthetic cluster, Leonia, with a real load history: thirty-one incidents, eight tenants, a Kubernetes upgrade in September. A run like this has no works, only fates; and no names either, because the creators write their names for themselves.

One incident, three fates

On the eighteenth of April Leonia had the worst incident of the year. The “scars” in the climate grew from 0.34 to 0.62 in a single night and by the end of May had almost reached one. Each creator felt it in their own way.

Writer number two felt the incident least of all. She had strength to spare, her despair never rose above a tenth, and in June her wonderful year began: her own Boldino autumn, Pushkin's most fertile season, in the middle of summer.

Writer number four felt it more strongly and met it already at the edge: despair close to one, a quarter of his strength left. After the incident an inner heaviness he had not had before began to accumulate every night. In May he fell silent, because he had come to repeat himself and realized it; in August he fell ill and recovered, in October he fell ill again, in November a third time, and he died, having managed on his last night to finish a piece that the critics accepted. While he was still alive, researchers found one of his old, unknown works in his notebooks and published it as well.

Composer number five, born on the night the atelier's first composer died, felt the incident more sharply than anyone: within two weeks the scars reached their limit for him. But he did not break. His despair grew but stayed bearable, and in eight nights his deferred idea gained 68 points of maturity, while the others gained three or four over the same nights. For him the storm became a time of ripening.

The lineage goes on

In the same year the atelier's first composer, a woman, died of despair on the eighty-fifth day, having finished a last piece that her inner critic never accepted. Four months later an early work was found in her notebooks and published posthumously; writer number two heard it. In October that writer herself fell silent, written out, and that same night a student appeared beside her; the next day he surpassed her. Composer number three lost someone close to him in November, writer number four, and a month later lived through his own wonderful year.

Nobody wrote any of this. There are only forces, climate, character, recognition, money, encounters, and the fates that come out of them.

How it is built

What the atelier is made of

The atelier is written in Python and is installed into a cluster with a single Helm chart. The chart brings ten resource types of its own in the group mnemosyne.art, the controller that manages them, and the gallery. Atelier describes the atelier itself and holds its lineage together with the fate engine, which can be resumed after a restart. Artist is the life of one creator: their catalogue, periods, teachers and creative state. Opus is a finished or abandoned work, Notebook is the notebook in which the whole search is written down, and Sketch is a single sketch. Review holds a critic's review, Biography a chapter of a biography, Leitmotif the image of a character, and AudienceLog the audience's journal. The heavy files, that is, scores, page images, plans and texts, are not put into the objects. They lie in a store addressed by SHA-256, and the object refers to them by hash, so that the reference does not break when the files move.

The controller is written with kopf, a Python framework for Kubernetes operators. It watches the Atelier object and keeps one long-lived deployment for it, which the code calls the life of the atelier (mnemo-life-…). Inside this pod several threads run in parallel, and each is busy with its own work. Every living creator has a thread of their own, in which they look for an idea, work, and then rest as long as their strength and character allow, from ten minutes to several hours. A separate thread reads the cluster every fifteen minutes. Another runs fate by the real calendar, bringing creators into the world, burying them and bringing them together. The critics and the chronicler write in their own thread when something appears that is worth writing about, and the studio renders accepted scores in its own thread without taking the composer's turn. While Korda was composing, the studio was finishing the recording of his previous symphony, and in the neighbouring thread Dorsz left her own work unfinished. Twenty minutes after “The Glover” was accepted, it already had a recording.

Next to the life stands the gallery, a separate read-only deployment. It mounts the volume with the works without write access, receives neither a service account token nor any keys, and rebuilds a static site every fifteen minutes.

In the chart values the owner of the cluster sets only the infrastructure: keys, models, resources, storage, the window of life and the list of what goes into the backup. The chart has no settings at all for ideas, schools, moods or plans, and this is deliberate.

How a composer arrives at a plan

Every step of the work is written into the notebook, so the path from the idea to the score can be traced in full.

First the creator writes the idea, that is, one sentence about what they want to do to the listener and a paragraph about what they want to say. The prompt from which the idea is born contains their self-portrait, their teachers, their inner weather, the poetics of their school and the climate of the cluster translated into feelings, without a single fact about the cluster. The idea is interrogated by the inner critic, who has his own notions of taste. If the answer does not satisfy him, the creator rewrites the idea, seeing both the previous version and the critic's objections. If the second version does not satisfy him either, the idea is put aside and turns into a problem that will mature until it comes back as an insight.

An accepted idea gets a method. The creator chooses a school from those they know or one of the schools founded in the atelier itself, and they can cross two schools, break one principle of their own school or declare a new one. At the same point they choose the form and the ensemble, seeing in which forms and for which ensembles their latest works were written. Then the undertones are written, that is, the shades the work has to carry, and the structural inclinations are chosen from the climate, which the creator's taste is free to weigh in its own way.

After that the composer writes the plan. The plan is JSON composed according to the grammar of the school or of the symphony, and the music in it is given not by notes but by decisions: which sections a movement consists of, in which metre and mode it goes, which instruments play, who leads the melody, who holds the background, and in which register and at what volume. The themes (theme:glove, theme:arabesque) are described separately in the same plan as sequences of pitches and durations, and time_scale stretches or compresses a theme for a single voice. This is how the flute, the clarinet and the cello play the same material at different tempi, and the result is the chase in free rhythms that Korda wrote about in his idea. Nothing the plan does not name appears in the score. This strictness had to be introduced after the first version of the grammar added the same harp to every symphony.

The work is built in cycles. First a sketch is written, the studio returns a score, an analysis and images of three pages for it, and the composer “listens” to the sketch through the analysis and the notes. The model knows that this is not real listening, and the prompt says so. Then up to three drafts are written, each with its own scale of revision: the first corrects form and dramaturgy, the second texture and voice leading, the third nuance. After each draft the inner critic gives a verdict, measuring the work by the measure of its school and the masters of that school, not by general taste. If the critic still asks for revisions and the drafts have run out, the decision is left to the creator: a confident one lets the work go over the critic's head, and an unsure one leaves it unfinished. The novelty of an accepted work is computed as the distance between its musical features and the features of the author's whole previous catalogue, and over time this figure adds up to how written out the author is.

How notes and sound come out of the plan

The plan turns into a score in the studio, and this is done with the ordinary tools of music engraving.

The score is built by music21, an MIT-licensed Python library for working with music notation. The school's grammar reads the plan and lays it out into music21 voices, bars and notes, after which the orchestration distributes the musical lines among the instruments. It takes into account ranges and transpositions, the breathing of the winds, bow changes in the strings, doublings and the division of sections into divisi, and an assignment that cannot be played stops the work with an error.

The finished score is written to MusicXML, an open music notation format that Sibelius, MuseScore, Finale and Dorico can read. Then musicxml2ly converts the MusicXML into LilyPond source, LilyPond produces a PDF from it, and pdftoppm cuts the single PDF into pages for the critic. Before the PDF is produced, the studio rereads the written MusicXML and checks it against the original score note for note. If there is a discrepancy anywhere at all, the work goes no further. Once this check caught a quarter tone lost when a trumpet part was transposed.

The sound is made from the same score. Each part is played by its own instance of sfizz, a free sampler for the SFZ format. SFZ is a text description of which sample of a live instrument sounds on which note, at which volume and with which technique. The samples come from open libraries: Sonatina Symphonic Orchestra and VSCO (Versilian Community Sample Library) for the orchestra, FreePats for the accordion, and VCSL for the Vietnamese zither đàn tranh. In all, the studio has 93 patches. The tracks are placed according to the seating of the orchestra, pass through convolution with the impulse response of the Jack Lyons Concert Hall at the University of York from the OpenAIR library, and are levelled to −20 LUFS with a peak limit. From this, ffmpeg makes FLAC and mp3 files, for the whole work and for each movement.

How a writer writes

A writer goes the same way, only in words. The idea and the critic's interrogation work for a writer in the same way as for a composer. The writer chooses a school from their library, which holds the teacher's school, what is being written around them and the schools they know from their reading, and while choosing they see how their latest pieces divide between schools. Every literary school has a described poetics: features that must be present in the text, devices that are forbidden in it, and the way such a text is to be read.

There are also up to three drafts. The inner critic reads each of them by the measure of the school and gives one of three verdicts: accept, rewrite, or a repetition of something already written. When he asks for a revision, he names exactly what has to be rewritten. The features of the finished text go into the author's catalogue in the same way as musical features do for a composer, and the novelty of the following pieces is measured against them.

Critics, the chronicler and the Translator

A published work is read by the outside critics. There are six of them, and each has a voice of their own: Mira writes laconically, Leon argues, Ada writes as a lyric poet, Pavel as a scholar, Iris as an ironist, and Nora confidingly, as if in a letter to someone close. Each has their own values, schools they love and dislike, a reputation, and a mood that also depends on the climate. Before reading, a critic has an expectation, and after reading the critic may change their mind. The author has to live with the verdict that was printed, and if it was crushing, the author's despair grows.

The chronicler writes biographies strictly from the events of a life and invents nothing. The Translator translates into Russian everything written in other languages and keeps the finished translations so as never to translate the same thing twice. He takes no part in the life of the atelier, does not age and influences no one. A translation into which a character from a foreign alphabet has slipped, a Chinese character in the middle of a Russian sentence, for example, is sent back.

How the models are connected

The atelier itself contains no model at all. It only calls models over HTTP, and which models are used and where they run is decided by the owner in the chart values. Each endpoint is described separately, with its address, model name, token limit, timeout and price, and then the endpoints are handed out to roles. This is the configuration of our atelier, with only the references to keys removed:

models:
  endpoints:
    opus:
      backend: anthropic
      base_url: https://api.anthropic.com/v1
      model: claude-opus-5-5
      vision: true
      max_tokens: 64000
      timeout: 1800
      price_in: 4      # dollars per million input tokens
      price_out: 20    # dollars per million output tokens
    sonnet:
      backend: anthropic
      base_url: https://api.anthropic.com/v1
      model: claude-sonnet-5-5
      vision: true
      max_tokens: 16000
      timeout: 600
      price_in: 2
      price_out: 10
  roles:
    artist: [opus]
    critic: [sonnet]
    chronicler: [sonnet]
    eye: [sonnet]
    translator: [sonnet]

There are five roles. The creator (artist) writes ideas, plans, drafts and texts. The critic (critic) judges: both the creator's inner critic, who interrogates the idea and gives verdicts on drafts, and the outside critics with their reviews work under this role. The chronicler (chronicler) writes biographies, the “eye” (eye) looks at the images of score pages, and the Translator (translator) translates. Which role is needed is determined by the name of the step: everything that begins with interrogation, listen: or verdict goes to the critic, everything to do with images goes to the “eye”, and the rest goes to the creator. Several endpoints can be listed for each role, and if the first is unavailable, the request goes to the second. Instead of an external API you can specify a model running in the cluster itself; the endpoint then has backend: openai, location: cluster and the address of a llama.cpp, vLLM or Ollama server.

Every step of the work is a separate request with a strict response schema. The schema is described in the code as a pydantic model. For example, this is the response of the inner critic listening to a composer's draft:

class Listening(BaseModel):
    verdict: Literal['accept', 'rewrite', 'repetition']
    reasons: str
    corrections: list[str]
    matters: bool                   # whether this work needs a chapter in the biography
    quality: float = Field(ge=0, le=1)

A JSON Schema is built from it and sent to the Anthropic API in the output_config field, so the model physically cannot answer with anything other than an object of this shape. The response is still checked once more by pydantic on the atelier's side, because the API does not accept some constraints, such as the range from 0 to 1 for the quality score, and they have to be cut out of the schema before sending. If the provider is overloaded or the network drops, the living atelier does not abandon the work but waits and repeats the request with pauses of 15, 30, 60, 120, 240 and 420 seconds, about a quarter of an hour in total. Responses are put into a cache keyed by the hash of the request, so a repeated identical request, for example after the pod restarts in the middle of a work, costs nothing. Every call is written into the notebook: which model answered, how many seconds it took and what it answered. The body of an error is never written down, because the provider may return the key and part of the prompt in it.

Everything to do with infrastructure has been cleaned out of the prompts. A creator is never told about the cluster, pods or incidents and receives only the climate translated into feelings. The models never receive the snapshot of the cluster at all: only artistic context goes to them.

Every call, with its token count and price, is written into the expense book spend.jsonl. From 15 November, when we turned it on, to 18 November the atelier spent 172 dollars: 111 went on 2133 Opus calls and 61 on 2545 Sonnet calls, and over this time 62 works were accepted, which comes to less than three dollars per accepted piece on average, not counting the rejected ones. For comparison, by measurements made during development, a full composer's work with a sketch and three drafts costs about seven dollars, and one critic's reading of score pages about thirty cents. The addon.budget value sets a monthly budget, and when it is used up, the creators wait for the start of the next month.

How the backup hides in the works

All the works and the artifacts of the process serve at the same time as a backup of the cluster, and this is how it is arranged.

Every fifteen minutes the sensor reads through the Kubernetes API the objects listed in the chart's inventory: namespaces, services, deployments, roles and their bindings, CronJobs, autoscaling, and anything the owner adds. What has been read is normalized, that is, cleaned of the fields the cluster sets by itself, and packed into a tar archive compressed with zstd. If something has changed, a new record is added to the state journal, either a full snapshot of the cluster or a difference from the previous record. A full snapshot is made at the very beginning and then after every thirty differences, so that the chain that has to be replayed during a restore does not grow too long. The atelier's own objects, that is, the creators, the works and the notebooks, never go into the backup. They are the fabric that carries it, not its content.

Each record is encrypted once, to the owner's public key. An ephemeral X25519 key is used for this, a key is derived from the shared secret through HKDF-SHA256, and the record is sealed with the ChaCha20-Poly1305 cipher together with its length and checksum. The encrypted record then has to be spread across many works in such a way that losing some of the works breaks nothing. If the record were simply cut into pieces and one piece handed to each work, the loss of any single piece would break the whole record. So the record is cut into K equal chunks (as Ceph calls such pieces), and repair chunks are added to them, each of which is the sum modulo two (XOR) of a random selection of the originals. Any number of repair chunks can be made, and each of them holds a little information about the whole record at once. To assemble the record again, it does not matter which chunks have survived: any K+16 of them, originals and repair chunks mixed, are enough, and the record is recovered by solving a system of linear equations, with a probability of failure below one in 65,536. This is redundancy coding of the same kind as erasure coding in Ceph or MinIO, and the variant of it in which any number of repair chunks can be made is called a fountain code. You can draw as much water from a fountain as you like, and it does not matter which drops end up in the bucket; in the same way, any number of chunks can be issued here, and the record can be assembled from any sufficiently large set of them. Such codes were invented for distributing files over unreliable networks, where some packets are inevitably lost. The atelier issues chunks with a margin, (K+16)/0.7 of them, so almost a third of the works carrying a record can be lost and the record will still be restored.

Here are the figures from the living atelier. The full snapshot of 15 November took 383 chunks. To survive the loss of three tenths of its carriers it needed 570 chunks, and all 570 have already been spread across the works. A difference written the same morning took only 10 chunks, and 38 were enough for it. The very first snapshot, made on 14 November while the atelier was still being put together, turned out to be the largest, at 2040 chunks, and of the 2938 it needs, 318 have been carried away so far. It does not stand in the way of a restore, which relies on the later full snapshots that have already been issued in full. A report on each such distribution lies in the work's folder, in a carry-*.json file.

The chunks are woven into the works in four ways.

In scores they are carried by the performance dynamics. In MusicXML, next to a dynamic marking such as p or mf, there can be a numeric value <sound dynamics>, which tells the player how loud to actually play. This number is a percentage of a standard forte: 100 means f, quieter markings get less, and for p it is somewhere in the low forties. Let us call the loudness the composer set with the marking the written level. The codec places additional points of such dynamics at equal intervals across the sounding notes, with the same marking, and lets each of them move away from the written level by no more than 1.875 percent in either direction, in steps of a quarter of a unit. Each point therefore has sixteen possible values and holds four bits. A reader of the score sees the same p as before, and a listener hears only barely noticeable fluctuations in delivery, of the kind any live performer has. Here is the beginning of one part from the first movement of “The Glover”:

<sound dynamics="44" />
...
<sound dynamics="42.125" />
...
<sound dynamics="42.125" />

The first number, 44, is the written level, the loudness the composer set with the p marking, and the next two points were placed by the codec. Both moved 1.875 below it, right to the edge of the tolerance, and together they carry eight bits. The margins of the notebook work in the same way: they are short phrases that do not need to sound, and all their dynamics are given over to data.

In texts the chunks are carried by spaces. Between two words there can be an ordinary space, a non-breaking space or a narrow non-breaking space. On screen they are almost indistinguishable, and the choice between them at each word boundary gives one and a half bits. This is how the composers' notebooks, the critics' reviews, the chapters of biographies and the manifestos of the schools carry data.

In the audience journals the chunks are carried by milliseconds. The time of each listening is recorded to the second with a spread of 0 to 255 milliseconds, and this spread, laid over a key mask, looks like an ordinary random offset but in fact stores a byte.

The bits themselves are distributed across these choices by an arithmetic coder that works in integers, without floating point, so that the layout is unambiguous on any machine. Each work also carries a catalogue, a JSON file from which the restore learns which chunks lie in the work and which record they belong to.

The owner's private key never enters the cluster. The atelier has a key of its own, with which it can seal its memory but cannot read it. Without the owner's key every work remains simply music, a text or a journal.

What had to be fixed

Most of the time went into making the creators stop writing the same thing. Nearly every failure looked the same: the works of different people came out alike, and each time the cause turned out to lie not in the models but in the way the world was built.

Everything in one quiet domestic register. The first works converged on one intonation, which always had some object, some moment and, without fail, a kitchen. It turned out that newcomers wrote their idea before any school at all had reached them, and our prompts carried one aesthetic for everyone. Now the poetics of the creator's own school enters the idea from the very start, the draft is written and reread by that school's measure, and every creator comes to the atelier with a life before art, a character and a manner of writing of their own.

One school for everyone. All the creators knew the same schools and chose whichever stood first in the list. Now each has a library of their own that grows with age. They see what is being written around them and relate to tradition as their temperament tells them, and a school that has filled a whole season begins to tire the critics.

Every composer's night ended in a rejection. On each round the critic sent the composer only his remarks and not the composer's own previous idea, so instead of revising what he had written, the composer started from nothing. Now both sides see the previous round, and a rejected idea is not lost but becomes the creator's problem, which goes on maturing in them.

The score did not obey the plan. The first symphony grammar wrote the same texture whatever the composer had in mind: always 4/4, the same harmonic cycle and the inevitable harp. The diagnosis was made by the composer himself, who wrote in his notebook that “the generator does not obey the plan.” In the second version of the grammar the plan became the skeleton of the score, in which metre, mode, layers of instruments, density and register are set, and everything the plan does not name stays silent.

Our rules instead of the laws of the form. We required every symphony to bring the same theme back in all its movements, although this was our habit and not a law of the genre. A composer was rejected three times for breaking this rule, after which we removed it.

Only symphonies, and only skaz. The composers wrote nothing but symphonies, and the writers were drawn into skaz, because the fashion around them and their own latest school reinforced each other. A creator simply did not see their own habit. First we showed it to them: a composer began to see the forms and ensembles of their latest works and every form the studio can play, and a writer began to see how their latest pieces divide between schools. One mirror turned out not to be enough. Having seen that their last three works were symphonies, both our composers, Korda and Dorsz, chose a symphony again, and Dorsz called her piece a cassation, that is, a light street serenade, and ordered a full orchestra from the studio. Then fatigue with a form entered the laws of the world, arranged in the same way as the critics' fatigue with a school: after three works in one form a composer's ear tires of it, and the next piece is written in another, whether a quartet, piano, choir, organ or a chamber ensemble. Which form to choose is still the composer's own decision.

The sound waited for the composer, and the composer waited for the sound. While the studio was rendering an old symphony, the composer could not start a new one, and the queue stood still for an hour. Now the studio has a life of its own and renders accepted works in a separate thread, alongside the creators.

Small things that ate the music. In the free sample library, the cymbals file had a comment with one slash instead of two, and because of it every symphony with cymbals was left without sound. Failed render attempts left their intermediate tracks behind, and one day eleven gigabytes of WAV filled the atelier's whole volume, so the following works had no sound until we enlarged the disk and taught a failed attempt to clean up after itself. Korda writes in just intonation, with quarter tones, and the trumpet is written a tone higher than it sounds. A B raised by a quarter tone became, after transposition, a C sharp plus fifty cents, and MusicXML keeps no cents. The studio, checking the written score against the sounding one, found the difference and dropped the work, and the composer lost a whole night. Now a quarter tone is written with its own accidental. The Translator once put a Chinese character into a Russian sentence. And the API classifier took the list of a creator's past works for an attempt to extract the model's reasoning and refused to answer, so past works are now named only by their title and first sentence.

Our atelier

To show how everything described above works in practice, what follows is an account of what happened in one particular atelier. It lives in a separate Kubernetes cluster, brought up as a tenant on our lab environment running Cozystack, with its own control plane, its own nodes and a 50 GB volume set aside for the works. It calls the models through the Anthropic API. The atelier has lived continuously since 14 November, and its gallery is open for anyone to read.

Who lives here

There are four creators in the atelier now. Each came with a life before art, which the creator writes for themselves in a self-portrait at birth, and with a character: openness, resilience, solitude, and sensitivity to different sides of the climate.

Anselm Korda writes in English and builds bridges: “By day I calculate loads, argue with riveters, and walk girders above brown water. By night I write music.” He is closed and resilient, keeps to his school, aleatoric counterpoint, in which each musician decides for himself when to come in, and he has already written six accepted symphonies.

Halina Dorsz is a Polish fisherwoman on a seiner. She “grew up between the docks and the fish market, among people who shout prices as if they were singing cantatas.” She is open to everything new, but fragile and lonely. She writes in the manner of Viennese classicism; the critics rejected two of her works as repetition, and her despair has begun to accumulate.

Tomé Arrais writes in Portuguese and grew up in an old quarter of tinsmiths and bookbinders, “in a big city that never looked at us.” He is open and resilient, and he moves from the fairy tale to skaz and on to the epistolary novel.

Ignazio Bellandi writes in Italian and “was born twice: the first time in a railway crossing house in the middle of the steppe, where the trains went by without stopping and the wind carried off even the words; the second time now, in mid-November, when I understood that I had to write or suffocate.” He has no teachers and keeps away from people. He writes skaz, magical realism, and prose in the school of Platonov.

Line Clear

Line Clear: read the translation

Many years later, facing the last page of the fire station's duty log, with his pen held two fingers above the paper, Ignazio Bellandi would remember that distant afternoon when his grandfather, who bore the same name, sat him on the stool in crossing house 214 and taught him to write the only sentence that family had ever written out in full. The crossing house stood at kilometre two hundred and fourteen of the line, counted from the capital, at a spot in the steppe where there was nothing to keep watch over but a level crossing that nobody crossed, and in those years the wind carried words away from the fifth syllable on. Short sentences arrived whole: “Bread.” “Shut it.” “Freight coming.” Long sentences left the mouth and reached the ear without their tails, and the tail went off eastward with the dust, so that for the twenty-two years the wind lasted the people of the line learned to speak four syllables at a time and to write down all the rest.

Grandfather Ignazio wrote. Every evening at nine, after the express that did not stop had gone by, he opened the log with the grey cloth cover, headed in red and ruled in blue, and wrote in the same slanting hand: “Crossing 214. Line clear. Nothing to report.” He did it for forty-one years, three months and nine days, fifteen thousand and seventy-six times, and never got a letter wrong, because he was a man who counted the doors before he slept and the poles before he woke, and who counted as an event only what had not already been written the day before. His wife, Nives Corradi, kept pigeons in a crooked cage on the roof of the crossing house, because pigeons were the one thing the wind could never manage to keep: it flung them against the sky for hours, turned them over, tore out their tail feathers, and they came back all the same, one by one, and she counted them aloud, four syllables to a number. Not the grandfather: he went on saying four syllables at a time until his death, which caught him at ten past three on a July afternoon, sitting on the stool with the log open in front of him; and since that morning, to save time, he had already written the evening's line, the last thing the railway received from him was that nothing had happened on the line.

She died in that room in the winter of eighty-nine, saying four syllables only, “Count the pigeons,” and the grandson, who along with his grandfather's name had taken on the habit of the doors and the poles, counted them that evening and every evening after.

They played scopa, Pace told the joke about the priest and the parrot that everyone knew down to the last comma, the shift chief Ruggeri slept sitting up, and at midnight one of them opened the duty log with the grey cloth cover, headed in red and ruled in blue, and wrote the line. The Pass of the Jars

Tomé Arrais, fairy tale, in Portuguese (original). In two valleys on either side of a mountain pass, every house keeps a clay jar and whispers into it what it never said to the one it should have said it to.

The Pass of the Jars: read the translation

Once upon a time, and not only once, for these things like to repeat themselves, there was a pass so high that the clouds asked leave to go through it. I tell it by the fire and not at the window; draw closer, your grace, and the door, leave it as it is, pulled to, which is how doors always stay. Whoever tells a tale adds a stitch to it, they say, but I add little: I change the words and keep the order, for the order is not mine. Now on that pass there was, and there is, a law, and the law is this, keep it in your pocket, for I will ask you for it at the end: what is buried speaks until someone answers it; and whoever answers keeps everything he heard and becomes the one to whom it was said. Nothing more. A short law, like the ones that hurt.

[1] On one side of the pass lay the valley of Sunrise and on the other the valley of Sunset, and in both valleys it was an old custom for every house to have its own clay jar. Into it, at night, with their mouth against the mouth of the clay, each person whispered what they had never said to the one they should have said it to; then they stopped it with cork and wax, climbed the cold pass and buried it, and came down to sleep like the just. In the house at the watermill in the valley of Sunrise, the one who carried the jar was the miller's wife. But the miller's wife, who had brought with her a daughter by another man, went one winter under the earth, without a jar and without warning, and there remained at the mill the miller, the wheel and the stepdaughter, who was small, thin and quiet, and whom the village called the Flour Girl, because she always went about white to her eyelashes.

[4] The first frost of that year came, and the miller, who had slept badly since his wife's death, set about sounding out his stepdaughter the way you feel a sack to see whether it has weevils. He asked her once, at supper: “Do you hear anything at night, girl?” He asked her another time, by the millstone: “Have you anything you've never told anyone?” And he asked her the third time, with the lamp already out: “Is there anyone you owe a word to?”

[5] And the girl, who did not know how to lie because no one had ever taught her, answered the first that she heard the wheel, the second that she had not, and the third: “I owe nothing to anyone, stepfather. I have no one to owe.” And the miller learned what he wanted to learn: that he had in his house an empty mouth, without debts, light as a jar not yet filled.

[8] That same week the cold bit so hard that the old jars, the grandparents' and great-grandparents', cracked under the earth of the pass, and the pits began to speak all at once. At first it was a murmur, like people praying in another room; then the wind took up the voices and brought them down the slopes, into one valley and into the other, and nobody slept any more. The dogs howled at the mountain, the children woke asking who was calling, the old men pulled their caps down over their ears, and it did no good. And the miller, on that sleepless night, sat in the dark with the house jar, put his mouth to it, whispered into it something long, stopped it with cork and wax, and set it at his stepdaughter's bedside while she slept. Let each one carry their own, says the custom. He did not carry his.

[9] Then the men of both valleys gathered at the big stone at the foot of the pass, those of Sunrise with the sun at their backs and those of Sunset with it in their faces, and the eldest said the thing as it was: “Someone has to go up and cover the pits, three nights running, before the pass wakes us to death.” They all fell silent, for each one knew what he had up there. It was the miller who spoke: “My stepdaughter will go. She owes nothing to anyone; the pass has nothing to say to her. And she'll take the mill's jar, which is still to be buried.”

[10] They all looked at the girl, white with flour beside the men, and she, who had never said no to anything, said: “I'll go.”

[11] At nightfall she tied her stepfather's jar to her back with a strap, took a little hoe and an oil lamp, and went up. Far or near, slow or quick, for on the mountain these things are not measured, she climbed by the goat path, and the higher she went the clearer the voices became, as when we come near a house where there is a feast and begin to make out the words. She made out none. Not yet.

[12] Right at the top, where the path comes undone and the earth does not know which way to run off, an old woman lay. She was sleeping stretched out along the line that divides the two valleys, half her body in Sunrise and half in Sunset, in a black shawl, with a goat beside her. She opened one eye and said: “Lie here with me till morning, child, but don't cross the line. And when the sky grows light, you tell me which side the sun will rise on.” The girl lay down on the line, with the jar under her head, and spent the whole night listening to the pits begging for she could not tell what, and trembling, and she did not move a finger to one side or the other. When the sky began to whiten, evenly, for up there the sky grows light evenly, the old woman asked: “Well? Which side?” And the girl answered: “I don't know, grandmother. I'm not the one who makes it rise.” The old woman laughed with the few teeth she had left, and said it was the first right answer she had been given in many years, because everyone answered her on the sun's behalf.

[14] Then she took from her shawl a cloth tied round a handful of earth, grey earth gathered from the line itself, and gave it to her. “This is no one's earth, neither one valley's nor the other's. A pinch on a pit and the pit falls silent. But listen well, for I say it only the way it is said: cover and don't answer, for what speaks under the earth is not for you.” The girl repeated it, because she was used to repeating what she was told: “I cover and don't answer, for what speaks under the earth is not for me.”

[15] The old woman said to the goat: “Go with her.” And the goat, which had a cracked bell, went ahead through the fog, tlim, tlam, tlim, over stones the girl could not see, as far as the field of pits, a flat bare ground just below the line, where grass does not take and the earth is always warm, even with frost all around. There the goat lay down, and the bell fell silent, and the girl was left alone with the voices.

[17] The first night she went from pit to pit with her ear to the ground, because the voices are many and you have to put your ear down to know where each one comes from. Under the first she heard: “Brother, I ate your bread in the winter of the snow and said it was the rat; you died in March, tell me it wasn't from the bread.” She threw a pinch on it and the pit fell silent. Under another: “Father, the vineyard I sold was yours; tell me you had given it to me, for I don't remember hearing it from you.” A pinch, and silence. And so on through the night, cover and don't answer, cover and don't answer, until morning. The second night the old woman said it to her again, the way it is said: “Cover and don't answer, for what speaks under the earth is not for you.” And she went down to the field again, and put her ear to a pit that was saying: “Wife, don't close the shutter tonight, I'll be back before the cock crows.” And the earth was so hot that it burned her ear, and when she lifted her head there was a dark mark on it, the size of a fingernail, shaped like a keyhole. She covered that one and covered others, “Son, tell me you didn't hear me that night through the wall,” “Neighbour, give me back my needle and tell me you found it yourself,” and when day broke she saw that the cloth was almost empty. One pinch was left.

[18] The third night the old woman said nothing to her, only laid her hand on the marked ear. The girl went down with the pinch shut in her fist and her stepfather's jar still on her back, for she had not yet found a place for it. And close to the line now, under a tiny pit, deeper and older than all the rest, she heard a child's voice, as near as if it were on the other side of a plank: “Mother, open the door for me, I can hear you on the other side.” The girl had the pinch in her hand. She had it. And instead of throwing it, she put her mouth to the earth and said: “I'm coming.” The pit fell silent under her ear, and the earth suddenly grew hot like the forehead of someone in a fever, and then cooled. And straight after, in the dark, there where there was no house nor wall nor any door at all, three small knocks were heard, knock, knock, knock, at the height of a knee. And then all the pits of the pass, those left uncovered and those already covered that had somehow unsaid the pinch, turned toward her, turned, I tell you, the way people in a room turn when someone at last answers, and began to beg of her. “Brother, tell me it wasn't from the bread.” “It wasn't from the bread,” she said. “Wife, don't close the shutter.” “I won't close it.” “Father, tell me you had given it to me.” “I gave it to you.” “Son, tell me you didn't hear.” “I heard nothing.” And so to the end of the night, I'm coming, I'm coming, I won't close it, I forgive you, I gave it to you, until the last pit fell silent and the pass was left mute as a locked church, and the wind went over it looking for someone and found no one. It was then that she buried her stepfather's jar, without hearing its voice, because it did not speak yet.

[19] That morning both valleys slept late, Sunrise and Sunset, a thing that had not happened in living memory. And when they woke, each at their own window, nobody remembered what they had buried. One man remembered that he had a brother, and that the brother had died in March, and nothing more; a widow left her shutter open without knowing why and slept well with it open. And if any of them, washing their face, felt a tiny unease at not remembering something, they fed it like a stray cat, and the unease went away, and they forgot it too. There was sun on both sides at once. At the mill, the miller sat down by the wheel to enjoy the warmth of the day, and everything was still, so still, when he heard at the door, down by the ground, three small knocks. He went to open it. There was no one there.

[20] The girl took leave of the old woman, who was already lying on the line again with her shawl over her, and went down. When she came to the place where the path splits in two, one for each valley, she stood still a good while, because she did not know which way her home was. Or she knew, but did not know whose was the wish to know. She went down to Sunrise out of habit, for feet have their habits.

[22] But not all the voices had stayed up there. They came with her, inside, wanting to get out through her mouth, and behind her on the path, as if someone small were following her barefoot, there came knocks on the stones, three, and three more, and three more. She ran, and fell, and ran again, until she reached the millrace, and she went into the water up to her knees, under the wheel, where the noise is so great you cannot hear yourself think. And the wheel, which always speaks and never asks for anything, spoke over everything, and the knocks did not cross the water, and the voices inside her fell asleep to the slap of the paddles like children being rocked. She stayed there until the moon came out, saved by the only mouth in the valley that had nothing to confess.

[23] When she came out of the water and knocked at the mill door, the miller came to open it with the lamp and did not know her. “Who are you, lady, at this hour?” he asked. For the flour had all come off her in the water, and what was left was a face he had not seen for two winters. And when she opened her mouth to say she was the stepdaughter, out came the voice of a grown woman, hoarse from too little sleep, and then another, an old man's, and then another, a child's, and she fell silent in fright, because she did not know, she truly did not know, which of them was hers. The neighbour women who came to look did not know her either, and one of them crossed herself.

[26] The next day the men of both valleys gathered again at the big stone, to find out who had silenced the pass, for such a thing deserved a reward. And the eldest, who was suspicious the way old men are who have slept well for the first time, said: “Whoever was up there, let them say the first word of the deepest pit, for not even I know what it is.” Many kept silent, some tried their luck, “brother,” “father,” “forgive me,” and all were wrong. The woman who had come from the mill, whom nobody knew, said softly, in a child's voice: “Mother.”

[27] Then the old man went straight to her and moved the hair away from her ear, and saw there the dark mark shaped like a keyhole, which is the mark the pass leaves on whoever puts their ear to it for three nights. “It's the Flour Girl,” he said. And the miller, hearing this, looked at her again and knew her, and was not glad.

[28] For at that instant the girl's mouth opened without her willing it, and out of it, before both valleys, in the voice of the miller's dead wife, came what the miller had whispered into the mill's jar and what she had buried without hearing, but which the earth, as you see, had handed over to her with all the rest: “Wife, you called me three nights and I said it was the wheel; don't call me any more.” And the girl, or the miller's wife, or whoever was standing there, answered him at once, him, in front of everyone: “I don't call you any more.” So the miller stood uncovered, he who had sent a child to carry what was his, and everyone knew that the heaviest thing in the mill's jar had not gone up the pass on the back that owed it.

[30] From that day no one in the two valleys answered the miller. Not as a punishment from the people, for the people remembered nothing, but because when he spoke all that could be heard was the wheel. He asked for bread at the shop and the shopkeeper looked at the millrace; he said good morning and the women turned their heads toward the water like people listening to rain. He lived many years like that, speaking and hearing the wheel for an answer, and no one ever called him again, by his name or by anything else.

[31] And the girl? A shepherd from the valley of Sunset, who had slept on the line guarding goats and had seen her lamp go down three nights, came to ask for her hand, and she said “I'm coming,” which was now the answer that came to her most readily. The wedding was held up on the pass, on the line itself, at a long table half in one valley and half in the other, and it was the first time that Sunrise and Sunset ate of the same bread; and they ate, and drank, and danced, and slept well, as they slept ever after. The old woman of the line was at the wedding, in her shawl, and said nothing. At the end of the feast, when no one was looking any more, the bride took from under her apron a tiny jar, the size of a closed fist, put her mouth to it, whispered something into it, stopped it with cork and wax and buried it on the line, neither on one side nor on the other. What she said I don't know. I won't tell it. They lived together many years, and the shepherd loved her, but sometimes he woke in the night and heard her answering someone in the dark, in a voice that was not the one he knew in her, “I won't close it,” “I forgive you,” “it wasn't from the bread,” and he lay very still, not knowing to whom. And sometimes, by day, on a mild afternoon, with the soup on the fire and the goats lying down, three small knocks came at the door of their house, at the height of a knee, and she got up and went to open it, and there was no one, and she sat down again as if there had been.

In by one door and out by another; whoever likes may tell another. But before you go to bed, your grace, you who sleep so well, take out of your pocket the law I gave you at the beginning and read it again, slowly. There are many pits, and many passes, and there is always a voice that begged someone for something you never got to hear. Someone, somewhere, answered it “I'm coming” in your place, and kept what was yours, and that is why you sleep. Who it was I cannot tell you. I know only that the door was left pulled to, as I asked. And if now, with the fire burning low and the whole house still, you hear down there, by the ground, three tiny knocks, don't go and open. Or go. It's no affair of mine any more.

Return to Sender

Tomé Arrais, epistolary novel, in Portuguese (original). A novel made only of letters that came back. When he chose the school, Arrais explained that the letter here is an event: it arrives at the wrong hour, it is refused, it gets stuck on the road, and “the truth lives in the gap between what is written, what is weighed and what is sent back.” And one more reason, this one about the times: “skaz around me is worn thin and puts the narrator's word at the centre,” while here the centre has to be the sealed envelope and the weight that does not add up.

Tinsmiths' Quarter, 3 November

Dear Madam,

You do not know me, and I ask you straight away not to be alarmed by the handwriting. It is a bookbinder's hand, and so it is straighter than the man who writes it. My name is Ilídio Saraiva and I have a workshop at the far end of Glue Lane. They call it that because of the smell, which is of hot fish; if you have ever boiled hide glue, madam, you will know what I mean and forgive me the detail.

I found your address on the back of an envelope. Half rubbed out, it is true: the name of the town can be read whole, Fonte Seca, the street can be guessed, and your name, Benvinda, came out darker than the rest, as though someone had written it twice. The envelope has a black border. It came to this house on a Saturday in March, when I was nine, and it was I who opened the door to the postman. My mother came behind me, wiping her hands, and said the sentence I heard from her only that once and that has never let go of me since: “In this house nobody dies by post.” And I, who was obedient out of fear and not out of virtue, gave it back.

Now, and this is where the thing twists, the envelope did not go. I found it three weeks ago, clearing out the drawer of the workbench that was my father's, under the bone folder he used to crease the gatherings. Closed, with sealing wax. I weighed it on the gold-leaf scale: twenty-three grams. A letter of condolence, madam, does not weigh twenty-three grams. There is something small and hard inside it, and when I shut the drawer I hear it roll from one corner to the other, slowly, like someone turning over in bed.

I have not opened it. I will not open it. I write only to ask whether you know who died, and whether that death was meant for us.

With the respect of one who knocks at the wrong door,
Ilídio Saraiva, bookbinder

P.S. The folder is of ox bone and has gone yellow where my father's thumb used to rest. My thumb does not fit there. I don't know why I tell you this.

Tinsmiths' Quarter, 9 February

Dona Benvinda,

All four have come back to me. They lie here on the bench in a row, like gatherings waiting for their covers. You refused them all, and I ought to be ashamed to go on. I am, but it is an old shame, inherited, and one more does not show.

The thing is that your postman, God keep him, who is a man of detail, writes on the back. In pencil, in a small hand, always in the same order. I copy them out for you, since you have not seen them:

“12 g. Ten twenty. Refused, but held it up to the light.”
“12 g. Nine forty. Wiped her hands on her apron before saying no.”
“13 g. Noon. Refused. Pressed the letter to her cheek, stamp side.”
“14 g. Late afternoon, candle lit in the upstairs window. Refused. Put her finger on the scale pan.”

Now listen, for this is what keeps me awake: I weighed them all before sending them, and they all left here at eleven grams. I tell myself it is the damp of the hills, that the paper drinks it. I say it in the same voice my mother used for saying the neighbour's dog howled because of the moon. But a bookbinder knows something the rest of the world does not: when a goatskin will not stick to the board, it is because something was left between the two. A hair, a grain, a nothing. You tear the skin off and there it is.

So I have decided. I am sending you the envelope. Not mine: yours. It goes as it came, with no letter, only the new address written over the old. If it is yours, hold it. If it is not, send it back, and I will put it in the drawer again and learn to sleep with it rolling.

Yours, out of stubbornness,
Ilídio Saraiva

P.S. I weighed it before I closed it into the big envelope. Twenty-three grams. It has not changed in thirty-one years. Mine change in a week.

Fonte Seca, 8 March

Most Excellent Inspector,

With all due respect, and with the hands Your Excellency will guess from the handwriting.

Yes, I wrote on the back. I have done it for twenty-six years with everything that comes back. It is not carelessness, Inspector, it is fear. When a letter is not received, someone has to say that it was there, and I do not want to be the last to have held it without a witness. I weigh because the scale is mine and because the number does not lie. I wrote down what they weighed. The bookbinder's letters gained weight in the hills. I cannot explain it, and that is why I noted it.

Of the bordered envelope I say what I saw, and only that. Dona Benvinda opened the door before I knocked. She did not reach out her hand from above, the way you take a thing; she put both hands underneath, the way you take bread out of the oven. She held it while the tower struck eleven, all the strokes and the rest of the hum after. She closed her eyes. Then she shook her head and gave it back to me. I felt at once that it was coming back light. Nothing rolled any more. I weighed it on the counter: nineteen. The wax is whole, with the seal impression. I give my word, for the little it is worth.

Simões, from whom I inherited the bag, told me once that some thirty years ago a black-bordered letter went out of that house to the capital, sent by the lady's mother. It never came back, and there was no notice of delivery. The old woman waited for it at the door every morning until the day they carried her out. I tell Your Excellency this not because it is service business, but because I have no one else to tell it to.

The next day the house woke with its shutters closed. The neighbour saw the lady go up the hill at daybreak with her hands closed one over the other, and says she has not come down yet.

I will not sign the report. I ask that I not be made to weigh anything more. I have fourteen months left.

Your Excellency's attentive and obliged servant,
Aurélio Pestana

P.S. The difference is four grams. I looked for them in the bag, in the lining of my coat and on the floor of the post office, on my knees. They are nowhere.

Inasmuch As

Ignazio Bellandi, the school of Platonov, in Italian (original). An official receiver makes an inventory of the belongings of an impoverished family living by the railway. Bellandi explained his choice of Platonov this way: “the crooked, official, wrong word says pain better than the smooth one”; Platonov also teaches you to tell in earnest about what seems comic, without hiding in irony.

Inasmuch As: read the translation

The official receiver came at the end of August, when the plain of Albinia was already yellow up to the embankment and the train for Grosseto went past the crossing house without stopping, because nobody there needed to leave any more. He was a man with shoes dusty to the ankle and a ledger under his arm, and he counted our things the way the drowned are counted after a flood, with respect and in a hurry: one wardrobe, five mattresses, one flowered curtain, one Ignis refrigerator, devoid of contents. My father followed him about in his vest, with the hole under the armpit through which the grey hair showed, and asked him questions, since nobody had ever explained to him how one becomes poor in the proper manner. “And what's missing, dottore, where do you write that down?” The receiver looked at the open refrigerator and said: “What is missing does not fall within my competence.” “And whose competence does it fall within?” “That will be seen at the hearing.” The court had already held forty hearings, and my father had attended them all in his Sunday shirt, feeling justice get into his knees like damp. When the receiver left, there remained under the wardrobe a sheet torn from his ledger: two columns, Assets and Liabilities, and at the bottom a printed line, Hearing No. ____. My father picked it up, smoothed it on his thigh with his palm and kept it, because a ruled sheet is already a beginning of order, even when there is nothing to put on it. That evening, after the bread soup, he got up, opened the refrigerator door and stood in the yellow light with his arms at his sides. “Your Excellency is hereby informed that as of today's date the butter is found to be unobtainable, inasmuch as.” There he stopped. The motor hummed a little longer, then fell silent, and in the silence the motor left behind, my mother, from behind the curtain, in the thick voice of the clerk of the court, said: “Adjourned.” My father shut the door, took the pencil and under the printed line wrote 41, with the care of someone numbering a file that others will have to consult. We little ones, in the dark of the shared bed, bit the pillow.

In the days that followed my father walked a great deal, given that walking was the last work nobody could seize from him. He went along the tracks to Marcucci's store, at the junction with the provincial road, where the plain ends against a wall of reeds, and asked for butter on credit, not for himself, but for the regularity of the family. Marcucci stood behind the counter with a pencil behind his ear and answered seriously, because he too was a man who was afraid of registers: “Credit is suspended pending further instructions.” “And who gives the instructions, Marcucci?” “When they come, they come.” “And where do they come? Here, or to us?” Marcucci did not know, and he gave him an empty piece of greaseproof paper that still smelled of butter, and my father brought it home folded in his breast pocket like a receipt. He came back at sunset with hunger in his hands, because hunger, in a man who walks, comes down from the stomach and settles in the fingers, which no longer know what to hold. In the evening he opened the door. Forty-two: the butter is found to be unobtainable, inasmuch as. The motor fell silent. “Adjourned.” Forty-three: the same, with the greaseproof paper laid on the empty shelf, as evidence for the prosecution. “Adjourned.” Forty-four, forty-five. My father wrote the numbers in the Liabilities column, one under the other, and the Assets column stayed white and clean like a sheet nobody has used. We bit the pillow not because it was a thing to laugh at, but because it was the only law still in force in the room, and whoever broke it left the others without a court. I was the smallest, and I bit so hard that one evening a feather was left between my teeth, and I kept it in my mouth until I fell asleep, so as not to make a noise even by spitting it out.

The crossing keeper's wife was called Ilva, and she had a vegetable garden that produced more than one family could eat, since the ground along the railway is fat with coal and with patience. One afternoon at the end of September she stopped my father at the level crossing and put into his hands a jar of pickles, peppers, little onions, cucumbers, all squeezed together in the vinegar like people in a railway carriage. “Take it, Bellandi, it's stuff left over.” My father held the jar with both hands and asked: “And what do I owe you?” “Nothing.” “Nothing can't be written down, signora.” “Then don't write it down.” He walked home slowly, looking at the jar against the light, because he did not understand how a thing left over for someone can reach the one who lacks it without passing through any office. That evening he did not go to the refrigerator at once. He sat on the bed in his vest with the jar on his knees, and I saw his whole life sitting in his shoulders, bent, as if he had to carry a new weight, the weight of having something. Then he got up, opened the door and set the jar on the middle shelf, beside the greaseproof paper. The light caught the little onions and made them white as teeth. “Your Excellency is hereby informed that as of today's date there is deposited,” he said, and stopped, because for things given he did not know the formula: the court had taught him only to ask and to have nothing. He stood there with his mouth open. The motor hummed. My father began again from the start, quietly, in his old voice: “Your Excellency is hereby informed that as of today's date the butter is found to be unobtainable, inasmuch as.” The motor fell silent. From behind the curtain my mother said: “Adjourned.” That was all she said, but in a smaller voice, almost her own. My father wrote 46 in the Liabilities column. After forty-six there were no more hearings. I took the ledger sheet myself, from the pocket of the Sunday shirt, and the printed line at the bottom, Hearing No. ____, is still to be filled in.

Now I live in Grosseto, above a courtyard where nothing burns, and on the roof I keep pigeons, which come home. At twenty past three, after a shift in which we played scopa [an Italian card game] at the station and nobody called, I opened my refrigerator. It is full. There is a two-hundred-and-fifty-gram block of butter, never opened, its gold paper still smooth at the corners. There is a tray of lasagne that the wife of Fanucci, the cook, has marked in felt pen: BELLANDI DON'T TOUCH 14/11. There is a bottle of spumante for my promotion to crew chief, which nobody celebrated, because the promotion came by post on a Tuesday; it lies on the bottom shelf, it is too long, and its neck sticks out past the edge and won't let the door close. On the door, held by a magnet from the Misericordia [a lay confraternity of mutual aid], is the sheet: Assets blank, Liabilities from 41 to 46 in my father's hand. The light of the refrigerator lay on my hands and made them someone else's, older. I did not decide to speak. I heard my mouth say, in his voice, which has lived in my throat since I stopped growing: “Your Excellency is hereby informed that as of today's date the butter is found to be unobtainable, inasmuch as.” The butter was there, in front of me, and I looked at the butter and waited for the motor's pause. With the door open the thermostat never reaches temperature and the compressor runs without a break; I know it from the course on building services, I know it from the kitchens we have gone into because of a forgotten pan. I did not move the bottle. I did not write 47 on the empty line, because there was nobody to tell me it was allowed. I stood with the light in my face and the spumante lying crosswise, the motor ran without a pause, and my mother's voice from behind the curtain was not there, inasmuch as.

Unravel

Tikhon Molchanov, “The Poetics of the Unentrusted”, in Russian (original). This story and the next one were written back in the runs on the synthetic cluster Leonia, which came up in the section about one incident and three fates. Molchanov wrote in Russian, and below is an English translation of his text.

The narrator keeps a workshop where old knitted things are unravelled back into yarn, and one day a woman brings him a green sweater with a cuff that has been cut. Molchanov founded the school in which the story is written himself, on the seventh night of his life, because, as he explained, “none of the schools on offer holds on to the main thing: closeness does not give the right to dispose of what belongs to another.” He ended the manifesto of the new school with three rules: “mend what you have damaged; ask where a pocket is needed; do not demand that your mending become someone's reconciliation”, and, as the laws of the atelier require, he immediately wrote the first piece in it. At the end of the text are the variants that the author did not strike out: that is how they were kept in his notebook.

Unravel: read the translation

On Tuesday a woman laid a green sweater in front of me and did not take her hands away at once. With one palm she held the shoulder, with the other the sleeve, as if the thing might slide off a level table. The sweater was heavy, with a wide ribbing at the bottom. On the left side, quite low, there was a dark patch pocket. A thread stuck out of the cuff.

“It’s already started,” she said.

I drew the sleeve toward me. The cuff had been cut near the seam; a few rows had come undone, and the rest had bunched up by the cut in small snags. The woman took hold of the sleeve with both hands. I pulled the loose end, and she let a little of the fabric go toward me.

That is how people hold a thing while someone is sewing it up.

“How much will it cost?”

“That depends on how much can be saved.”

She looked at the sweater, then at the bags under my table. In a clear bag lay short red threads, in a blue one a loose beige wool tangled with a darker one. Two more bags stood against the wall, pinned there by an empty basin. With my foot I pushed the blue one further in.

“Sell the yarn,” she said. “Keep the money for the work. If it isn’t enough, I’ll add more.”

There was no room on the table for the whole sweater. I had to move the scales to the windowsill and put the box of buttons on the floor. The woman held the edge while I cleared the board. Under her nails there were thin white lines of flour or chalk. On the thumb of her right hand the skin had split beside the nail and been smeared with something shiny.

She said her name was Nina Sergeyevna.

“Do you need a receipt?” I asked.

“Let’s have one.”

I wrote in her name, the color of the item and the word “unravel.” In the space for the amount I put a dash.

“Payment after weighing. I don’t count wet wool. It still has to dry, and more will fall out of it.”

“All right.”

“And buyers don’t pay for scraps.”

She nodded so quickly that it was as if I were the one offering her too much.

The room behind the shop was narrow. If someone sat at the table, you could get to the sink only sideways. Customers had long since stopped washing their hands at the sink: the door here was covered by a curtain, and behind it my half began. Wooden frames for skeins hung on the wall, and a faded towel on a nail. The window looked out on the passage between our building and the bakery. Even on a clear day you needed a light here by noon, and in November the day did not venture in at all.

Nina Sergeyevna took off her coat. Under it was a cardigan buttoned on the wrong buttons. She noticed me looking, ran her palm down her stomach and rebuttoned the bottom two.

“May I sit for a while?”

I pulled a second stool out from under the table.

She put her bag on her knees but kept her hands on the sweater.

I was not a craftsman, though the owner of the shop sometimes told customers, “We have a craftsman back there, ask him.” At first I corrected her. Then I stopped coming out from behind the curtain when the talk was about me, and there was no one left to correct.

Olga gave me the knitted things that hadn’t sold. Some had been left on consignment and not collected when the time ran out; others were brought in by the sackful from the start. For things that could still be put out for sale, she settled with the owners. The ones handed over for good, to be taken apart, she entered in my notebook by weight. I unpicked the seams, took off zippers and buttons, looked for where the knitting began. Then I unraveled it, tied the yarn into skeins, washed, dried and rewound it.

Two thirds of the takings were mine. Water, space and bags counted as Olga’s share. For days when nothing sold, nobody paid anybody.

Decent wool came along rarely. More often people brought cardigans cut out of knitted fabric: you unravel one row and get a thread as long as the cardigan is wide, the next row the same. I kept these lengths separately, though I knew in advance that nobody would take them. Now and then someone asked for stuffing for a pillow, but wanted it for free. Olga allowed me to give away the worst scraps and got angry if I started explaining to the customer how much work was in the bag.

“He doesn’t need your work in a pillow,” she would say. “He needs bulk.”

There was less and less room under the table.

Once I gave her back a child’s vest that had felted at the neck. I said that wool like that wouldn’t unravel. The vest was small, with two cables down the front, and its owner had sewn a white strip with a name onto the inside. Three letters were left on the strip. I couldn’t find which edge to start from, and for some reason I couldn’t admit it. Olga took the vest back into the shop, to the box of cheap things. A week later someone took it along with two sheets.

Since then I had especially liked people who said, as they handed a thing over, “You know best.”

Nina Sergeyevna didn’t say it. But she didn’t ask how long I had worked here, didn’t test the scissors, and didn’t even warn me that the sweater was hand-knitted. She turned the cuff toward me and waited.

I took out a few damaged rows. The yarn came away with a dry crackle. At the folds the wool was duller; near the seam it kept a deep green. Unraveling the ribbing from the bottom turned out to be awkward: the stitches caught on one another, and I had to pull the end through each one. I explained that first I would take the seams apart and start the sleeve from the top.

“It’s all the same to me how you find it easier,” she said.

That was almost what I liked to hear.

Behind the curtain Olga was arguing with a man about a jacket. He had brought it in a week ago and now claimed the price on the tag had been different. While they looked for the record, I spread out the shoulders of the sweater. There were stripes on the back from the drying rack. Nina Sergeyevna watched my fingers.

“Did you wash it recently?”

“The day before yesterday. It takes a long time to dry.”

“Good wool.”

“Yes.”

She said it without pleasure.

At first I decided the sweater had belonged to her husband. It was broad, and the sleeves had stretched at the elbows. Nina Sergeyevna took a pair of men’s trousers out of her bag, turned up the bottom of one leg and began looking in the lining for a needle. I pushed the box of thread toward her.

“I have my own,” she said. “Only I could use some light.”

I switched our stools. She ended up closer to the window. As she sat down, the green sleeve slipped off the table into her lap; she picked it up at once and laid it beside me.

“Your husband’s?” I asked.

“The trousers? Yes.”

I touched the sweater.

“That’s my daughter’s.”

She was threading the needle and couldn’t see my face.

“She doesn’t need it anymore?”

“There’s a lot she doesn’t need, and she wears it.”

I wrote the number on the receipt, put it under the scissors and set to work on the seam. The words seemed clear enough. An old sweater, an unneeded sweater, wool for sale. I already had orders lined up, and I was working out who the green would suit.

“When did you knit it?”

“Four years ago now. I sat over it for three months. In the evenings. Back then there was work too, and everything else.”

She turned up the trouser leg and pressed the fold with her nail.

“I want those three months back.”

“Hardly anyone knits yarn that loosely now,” I said. “Usually it’s tight, so the wind doesn’t get through.”

That was an answer not to her words but to the thing. She went on, though, as if I had agreed.

“I keep reminding her. Put it on, I knitted it for you. Don’t throw it around, I sat over it. Yesterday I heard myself. There I am again: I spent three months… And she says, ‘I wear it when you’re not around, too.’”

Nina Sergeyevna stopped with the needle above the fold.

“Well, there it is. I’ve lived to see it. That’s how she answers now.”

She pulled the thread, but the knot didn’t hold in the fabric. She had to start again.

“Maybe she just wears it,” I said.

“I know how she said it.”

I didn’t argue. I was pleased that a woman who had come in half an hour ago was talking about this with me and not with Olga behind the curtain. I left the scissors lying where they were and waited while she tied the knot. She blew on the end of the thread.

“This morning I took it off the rack. She’s on shift. I thought I’d quickly unravel it myself. I found the end, made a cut. And then I keep putting it on my arm. I hold it here,” she showed her wrist, “and think: it could be finished. It still could.”

“It could have been.”

“That’s why I brought it to you.”

Now I looked up.

She made the first stitch in the trousers. The stitch came out long, and she pulled the needle back.

“You won’t do half a day of it and then give up, will you? You have to earn a living too.”

Behind the curtain the front door banged. The man with the jacket had left. Olga asked whether we wanted tea. Nina Sergeyevna answered for both of us:

“No, thank you.”

I picked up the scissors and slipped them under the joining thread of the shoulder.

I had three gray vests of the same size, brought from a shoe repair shop that had closed. One woman must have knitted them: on all of them the stitches were decreased the same way, and the same knot stuck out near the right shoulder. The first one I had taken apart the week before. It gave a hundred and eighty grams of clean dry yarn. On Monday Tamara bought it and left seven hundred rubles for the next six hundred grams by Thursday.

The six hundred were to come from the two remaining vests and my stock of gray wool. Tamara was going to knit a long sleeveless top for her sister. From the first batch she had already cast on the back. I showed her the other vests, and we agreed that the shade barely wandered.

Now they lay on the top shelf, under the box of buttons, and the green sweater took up the table. I decided I would manage both. The sleeve came easily; the joining seams gave too. Nina Sergeyevna hemmed one trouser leg and laid the other against it.

“He can’t have the edge catching,” she said. “He uses a walker. He gets angry himself when I fix things. And then he steps on it and calls me.”

She measured off the fold and pinned it. In her bag was a loaf of bread wrapped in a towel. I saw the end of the loaf every time she bent for the scissors.

“And what will you do in the evenings?” I asked.

“What do you mean?”

“Well, when they’re free.”

She looked at the trousers on her knees.

“This, for now.”

By lunchtime I had separated the sleeve and unraveled it almost to the cuff. A big curly bundle lay on the back of the stool. Now and then Nina Sergeyevna ran her fingers over it. She wasn’t checking it or untangling it, just touching it, the way people touch the edge of a tablecloth during a conversation.

Under the arm the wool had felted. The seam thread, the stitches of the sleeve and the edge of the body had got mixed up there. I spent a long time separating them with a needle, then got tired. I decided to cut out the dense little piece whole. Nina Sergeyevna was folding the trousers just then. I squeezed the fabric, slid the lower blade under it and heard a short crunch.

Three stitches opened up on the body.

I held them down with my thumb. If the sweater was going to be unraveled anyway, nothing terrible had happened. That is what I told myself, though there was a lot of work left before the unraveling was done, and now the loose ends would have to be tied.

“Is that it?” Nina Sergeyevna asked.

“No, it’ll be a long while yet.”

“I have to go home. He won’t eat lunch without me.”

She put on her coat, took the receipt and counted the money in her purse.

“How much should I leave now?”

“Later. I don’t know the yield yet.”

At the door she came back to the table.

“Don’t put it off, now.”

“I’ll do it.”

I wanted her to leave at ease. She took her bag in both hands and went through the shop, the trousers in her bag brushing the skirts that hung by the aisle.

The pocket got in the way of spreading out the bottom edge. I slipped the scissors under the thread it was sewn on with and cut it in several places. It could have been unpicked more neatly. Crumbs spilled onto the table, a dry bit of tangerine peel, a white thread. A small safety pin was stuck in the corner of the pocket.

I brushed the crumbs into my palm. The sweater smelled of wet wool, though it was already dry, and of something sweet from the pocket.

Before this I had already called it unneeded twice: once aloud, telling Olga why I needed one more thing on the table, and once in a note on the back of the receipt.

The daughter came when Olga was already taking the price tags off the rack outside. First I heard her question, then the owner’s silence. Usually Olga answered at once, even if she knew nothing.

The curtain was drawn aside.

The girl wore a short jacket over a thin cardigan. Wet snow pellets sat on the collar of the jacket. She held the receipt by the corner, between two fingers.

“Where is it?”

I stood up. The green sweater lay in front of me, folded so that the cut under the arm was on the inside. Next to it lay the sleeve, no longer a sleeve, and the pocket.

She went to the table, unfolded the body and held it up by the shoulders. The empty armhole sagged. The girl lowered the thing, touched the other sleeve, then picked up the pocket.

“What was this for?”

“To get to the edge.”

“What edge?”

I showed her.

“But it’s sewn on separately. You could have left it alone.”

“For a full taking apart I’d have had to anyway.”

“I didn’t ask for a full taking apart.”

She said it without raising her voice, and for the first time I heard in the words “a full taking apart” something I hadn’t heard that morning.

“Your mother brought…”

“I know. I found the slip at her place. She said she’d given it in to have a sleeve taken off.”

“No, she ordered the whole sweater unraveled.”

The girl looked at the receipt. There was only one word on it, which I had written myself.

“Give it back.”

I took the bundle from the back of the stool.

“This is yours too. I haven’t washed it yet.”

She didn’t hold out her hand. She stood holding the pocket and looking at the curly wool.

Olga stayed by the curtain. I wanted her to step in, to explain at least that the thing had been brought in willingly, but she asked the girl:

“Do you need a bag?”

“Yes.”

Only then did I notice she was trembling. Not crying, not choking with anger: her shoulders were trembling finely under the jacket.

“I can fix it,” I said.

The girl turned her face to me.

“Can you knit?”

“I’ll find someone. The sleeve is all there in the yarn. Almost all.”

“And this?”

She spread the cut under the arm. I saw her finger on the other side of the fabric.

“I caught that. When I was separating the felted place.”

Olga went for a bag. I was left alone before the girl and the green hole.

“By Friday,” I said. “I’ll try to have it by Friday.”

“I have work tomorrow.”

“We can find you something here.”

She glanced back at the curtain, behind which the clothes hung.

“I came to get my own.”

I didn’t offer anything more.

Her name was Asya. She wrote it herself on the back of the receipt, where I had noted the unneeded thing. She crossed out my note with a single line and held the pencil over the paper for a long time.

“I’ll take the pocket now. And you don’t cut anything else.”

“All right.”

“You don’t unravel the other sleeve. Or the bottom. I don’t need it all to match afterward. I need to wear it.”

“Understood.”

“And you don’t give it to my mother. Even if she comes.”

I wrote that down. Asya didn’t check every word, only moved the paper closer to the light.

“I can leave it till Friday. On Friday I have a short shift. I’ll come by after two.”

She spoke as if she were dividing work among people she had known a long time and from whom she expected no particular care. I wanted to explain that nothing like this had happened to me with other people’s things before. But that wouldn’t have been quite true either: other people’s things passed through my hands all the time, and most often I didn’t know who else considered them theirs.

“You don’t need to pay anything,” I said.

“I hadn’t asked yet.”

She pulled her jacket tighter and did up the top button. Olga brought a thick men’s jumper from a shelf.

“Take this till Friday. It’s wide, but it’ll fit under your jacket. No deposit needed.”

Asya touched the collar.

“Is it scratchy?”

“A little. Over a cardigan it’s bearable.”

“Can I bring it back afterward?”

“You can.”

Asya took off her jacket and pulled on the jumper. One arm got stuck in the sleeve, and Olga helped her straighten the lining of the cardigan. For a moment both of them were taken up only with this: an elbow, a turned-out edge, someone else’s wide ribbing.

In the doorway Asya turned around.

“Did she tell you I don’t wear it?”

“She said you wear things you don’t need.”

“Ah.”

She fingered the pocket in her hand.

“Yesterday I told her I wear it without her, too. So she’d leave me alone. Not so she’d unravel it.”

It was the same phrase I had already heard that morning. Asya stuffed the pocket into her bag and left.

I sat down, took the receipt and for the first time read carefully everything I had written. There wasn’t enough room on the back. I could have started a new sheet, neat, without crossings-out, but I didn’t. Olga moved the stool back against the wall.

“Tomorrow go to Vera first thing,” she said. “You won’t knit this yourselves.”

“I know.”

“I don’t mean you. I mean in general.”

She took the scales off the windowsill, set them in front of me and closed the door to the shop. I was left with the wool in my lap. In two hours it had grown noticeably heavier, though the scales wouldn’t have shown it.

I unwound the bundle back into a skein. In places the yarn caught on my skin. It needed washing, drying, straightening, and before that all the broken stitches on the body had to be found and secured. I didn’t know in what order to take it on.

Two gray vests lay on the shelf. I had promised Tamara six hundred grams by Thursday.

Wednesday

Vera Ilyinichna came at half past eight, before the shop opened. Olga had met her on the way and brought her through the back yard. Vera was carrying a string bag of jars and was displeased that we were standing in the doorway.

“It’s slippery out there, and you’re blocking the way.”

I moved the bucket.

She laid out the sweater, took off her glasses, put them on again. She looked at the cut longer than at the unraveled sleeve.

“Why was this cut?”

“It had felted.”

“I can see it felted. Why from that side?”

I showed her how I had held the fabric. She took the scissors, repeated the movement in the air and put them further away.

“You should have opened the seam first. It’s a double thread here. Pull out one, and the other would have loosened.”

“I was in a hurry.”

“Now I’ll be the one who has to hurry.”

She said it without any particular meaning, simply stating how things stood. She had an unfinished cardigan back of her own waiting, for a woman who was leaving on Saturday. Vera had also promised her daughter to sit with her grandson on Thursday afternoon. I listened and couldn’t tell whether she had agreed or was refusing.

“It has to be by Friday after two,” I said.

“I heard.”

She took the whole sleeve, counted a few rows, measured the width with her palm, then asked for a tape measure. Mine turned out to be in the box under the buttons. Vera told me to bring paper.

“This one is longer than it was. It stretched at the elbow. If I knit to match it, the new one will stretch later too. The owner needs to be asked how she wants to wear it.”

“She said not to change anything else.”

“I’m talking about the new sleeve. It doesn’t exist yet.”

Again I felt like a man being told the simplest things. I wanted to say that at least I knew how to unravel. Instead I straightened out the skein.

Vera ran the yarn through her fingers.

“Wash it first. In warm water, no sudden change. Don’t rub. And don’t wring it into a rope, or I’ll be fighting your rope afterward. Roll it in a towel. Dry it loose. We’ll see by evening.”

“And the hole?”

“The ends are short. Not everything can be picked up. I’ll secure it, draw it together. On the inside it’ll be thicker.”

“Can it be made invisible?”

“To you or to her?”

I fell silent.

“Under the arm it won’t show,” she added. “But she’ll feel it. Tell her straight away.”

For the sleeve, the assembly and the cut she asked two thousand two hundred. I was to prepare the yarn myself. If it turned out to be too little, extra yarn would be separate.

I didn’t have two thousand two hundred rubles to spare.

“Agreed,” I said.

Vera covered the sweater with a clean sheet so that lint and dust from the other things wouldn’t land on it. Then she took her jars and went for milk. A few minutes later she came back for the tape measure, which she had put in her pocket by mistake.

“And don’t touch the collar. It’s good.”

I hadn’t meant to touch the collar, but after she left I checked all the same whether I had done something to it the day before.

When I started washing the green wool, Nina Sergeyevna came. She had her purse in her hands. She didn’t take off her coat.

“Did my daughter come by?”

“She did.”

“Did she take it?”

I pointed at the table. The sweater lay under the sheet, and she didn’t realize at once that it was the sweater.

“I thought you’d finished.”

“She asked for it to be restored.”

“Restored how?”

I turned back the sheet. Nina Sergeyevna looked at the empty armhole, at the other sleeve. I had pinned the cut, as Vera told me, and it looked smaller than it was.

“But I asked for it to be unraveled,” she said.

“And she’s asking for it back.”

“I’m paying for the work.”

The smell of wet wool rose slowly from the basin. I kept my hands over the water so as not to drip on the table.

“You won’t pay for this. I’m the one who cut it.”

“Then what do I owe?”

I dried my hands.

“Nothing for now.”

“Don’t give me ‘nothing.’ How long did you work yesterday?”

She opened her purse. Between the bills lay a slip of paper folded in half, with a list. I saw the word “bread” and three figures under it.

“Nina Sergeyevna, I won’t take money for the unraveling.”

She closed the purse, then opened it again. She took out the slip, smoothed it and put it back.

“She isn’t speaking to me. This morning she came for her cup. She took the cup, and not a word to me.”

I knew nothing about the cup. Yesterday, listening to her, I would have asked how long the daughter had lived on her own, how often she came by, how she helped with her father. Now every question seemed like the beginning of a job she might hand me next.

“She’ll come today to measure for the sleeve,” I said. “If she has time.”

“I’ll wait.”

“She doesn’t know you’re here.”

Nina Sergeyevna looked at me with dislike.

“I’m not a stranger.”

“I know.”

“Then why do you talk like that?”

I had no good answer. I wrung the edge of the towel over the basin, though there was no need to.

She went to the table and lifted the whole sleeve. She put her palm into the cuff and smoothed the ribbing. Then she took her hand out.

“I didn’t want her bowing to me for it for another five years.”

“She didn’t say yesterday that she bows to you.”

“There’s a lot you weren’t told.”

That was fair, and I couldn’t use the fairness of her words for anything useful.

Nina Sergeyevna stood by the stool. I offered her a seat, but she shook her head.

“If she comes, tell her…”

She stopped. I waited.

“Nothing. I’ll do it myself.”

In the doorway she asked where the pocket was. I said Asya had taken it. Nina Sergeyevna nodded and went out. The purse was still in her hand.

Asya came before lunch with a bundle under her arm. In the bundle was Olga’s jumper. She said she had put it on in the morning but the collar rubbed her neck, and she asked for something else for the time being. Olga went to look.

Vera hadn’t come back yet. I showed Asya the note with the measurements and explained about the length. She pulled the remaining sleeve onto her arm, holding the body in front of her chest. The cuff covered her knuckles.

“This is how I like it.”

“It may stretch later.”

“Let it. I turn it up if it gets in the way.”

“The new one will be different after washing. Softer.”

“As long as it isn’t elbow-length.”

She laughed briefly, more from irritation. I wrote down the length from the shoulder. Then I told her about the seam under the arm.

“How thick?”

“I don’t know yet. Vera Ilyinichna said you’ll feel it.”

“And without it?”

“Part of the body would have to be unraveled and reknitted. That takes longer. And you forbade it.”

“I forbade you to cut anything else. If it needs reknitting, you should have said so yesterday.”

“Yesterday I didn’t know.”

Asya lowered the sweater onto the table.

“Then why did you promise it by Friday?”

Behind the curtain Olga was moving hangers. They knocked against each other, and I very much wanted her to come back in.

“So that you’d leave it,” I said.

Asya looked at me, then at the skeins hung over the basin.

“I left it because I have nothing to work in. There’s a draft from the door. In my kiosk. If I had two more, I’d take it right now and look for someone to do it myself.”

She spoke without pauses, for the first time not waiting for my answer.

“I don’t need you to calm me down. I need to understand what I’ll get on Friday. One sleeve different. A seam under the arm. Right?”

“Right.”

“Can the seam be redone later?”

“We can show it to Vera. I don’t want to make promises for her again.”

“We’ll show her. For now let her do what she has time for. Only don’t touch the bottom. I need it for the length.”

I asked whether she worked at the end of the line. Yesterday Nina Sergeyevna had mentioned a kiosk, but I had listened to her story as if the kiosk were only the place where her daughter was absent from home.

“At the far one, by the turnaround.”

“It’s cold there.”

“It varies.”

Olga brought a buttoned sleeveless top and a long scarf. Asya tried the top on over her cardigan, turned to the window, raised her arms. It was tight across the chest, but under a jacket there was no need to button it.

“It’ll do till Friday,” she said.

Before leaving, Asya took the pocket out of her bag. In one corner was the pin, which I hadn’t noticed when I gave it back.

“Did a key fall out here?”

“What key?”

“To the toilet. A little one, on a string.”

We looked under the table, in the basin, in the bag of crumbs. There was no key. Asya said she would check at home. I saw her to the curtain and asked whether she had worn the sweater often.

She stopped.

“Would that have helped yesterday?”

I stepped back so she could pass.

The Sunday before these four days

Asya opened the kiosk at seven forty. Two buses stood at the end of the line; between them a man was tipping gray water out of a bucket. There had been a frost in the night. By the kiosk step lay a flat slab of ice with a cigarette butt frozen into it; Asya pushed it aside with her toe so she wouldn’t step on it later.

Inside it smelled of cardboard, sweet buns and a damp rag. The other shift had left the rag in the sink. Asya hung it on the edge of the bucket and wrote on a scrap of paper: “Lyuba, wring it out, please.” She looked at the note and added at the bottom: “It smells in the morning.” Then she put the scrap under the sugar bowl so it wouldn’t blow away.

The chair was rocking again. Under the short leg was a piece of cardboard that had gone soggy back on Friday. Asya pulled it out, tore a dry strip off a box of biscuits and folded it in four. She sat down and tested it. It was better, though now the chair leaned slightly toward the counter.

She wore the sweater over a cotton shirt. She hung her jacket on the nail by the door. She had to turn up the left cuff: the edge kept brushing the paper cups. She left the right one as it was, because she held her mug in her right hand more often, and the mug stayed warm a long time.

The first to come was a driver who always asked for the softest bun and got angry if Asya felt it through the bag.

“How else am I supposed to know?” she asked.

“You can see it.”

She picked the palest one. The driver approved, asked for two napkins, then came back for a third.

By nine it was warmer in the kiosk. Asya took off her scarf and ate half an egg standing over an open drawer. In the drawer were bags, spoons and her bread. Crumbs fell onto a bag; she shook them into her palm and ate those too. She didn’t get to the second half of the egg: two road workers came for tea.

When she sat down, the low pocket of the sweater ended up right under her left palm. In it was the toilet key on a blue string. Asya felt for it before going round behind the kiosk, and sometimes it was by this movement that she noticed she had been holding on for a long time.

There was also some tangerine peel in the pocket. Lyuba’s little nephew had put it there when he came by during the week: he wanted to make a scented pouch, and no pouch could be found. Asya had thrown out most of it, but one piece had got stuck by the seam. She took it out, sniffed it and threw it in the bucket.

Around noon a woman in a red hat asked her to write down three rubles against her name. Asya said three rubles didn’t need writing down. The woman took offense and left a glove on the counter as a pledge. After that Asya spent a long time looking at the stranger’s glove next to the buns, not knowing where to put it.

Her mother didn’t call that day. Asya remembered her when she saw a thin trace of flour on her sleeve. The same trace was left on the table at home after pirozhki. She brushed off the flour but didn’t brush down the whole sweater: just then a draft came in from the door behind her.

After her shift Asya brought the sweater to her mother’s to be washed. Her mother had a good drying rack, while in Asya’s rented room a wide garment fit only across the backs of two chairs and got in her neighbor’s way.

“Did you check the pockets?” her mother asked.

Asya took out the key, put it on the saucer by the bread bin and said:

“Yes.”

Later she took the key back along with some coins, but not right away. First she and her mother looked for where the saucepan lid had got to, and her father called from the other room asking them not to clatter: he had only just dozed off.

By Wednesday evening the green wool was still damp. I hung the skeins further apart, put a dry basin under them and went for Vera Ilyinichna. She lived across the yard, on the ground floor. She opened the door with flour on her forearm.

“I told you I’d come by.”

“It’s almost dry.”

“Almost doesn’t suit me. If I knit it damp, it’ll dry and the size will go.”

I went back. Olga was closing the shop. On my table lay the money I had earned in two days: four hundred and eighty rubles and some change. In a box under the shelf I kept seven hundred grams of good dark blue yarn. I had been saving it to sell in December, when people start looking for wool for presents. It was soft, without knots, all one shade. It could fetch more than everything I had unraveled in the last week and a half.

“Tamara’s coming tomorrow,” said Olga.

“I remember.”

“What will you tell her?”

“That I didn’t make it.”

“She asked back on Monday whether Thursday was certain. I heard.”

I took one gray vest down from the shelf. The familiar knot stuck out on the shoulder. I unpicked the side seam and separated the front. Gray threads settled on the stool; green ones hung over the basin. I wanted to quickly do something I was good at.

When Olga left, you could hear in the room the water dripping from the wet towel. I worked until I began missing snags and breaking the yarn where I could have pulled more carefully. I gathered the skein I had and weighed it: seventy grams. There were still the back, the ribbings and the whole second vest.

Seventy grams didn’t bring Thursday close enough to be worth spoiling the rest. I put the vest in the box, covered the table and went home.

At the shop door I had to search a long time for the key. It turned out to be in the other pocket. I stood in the wet snow, sorting through change, and was angry at Asya for her question, whether it would have helped yesterday to know she wore the sweater. It would have, I wanted to answer. Of course it would have. I had to learn it from somewhere.

Then I found the key and saw green fluff on my thumb. That morning Nina Sergeyevna had said: my daughter’s. There had been somewhere to learn that from as well.

I didn’t lock the door on the first try. The bottom corner had swollen, and I had to lift it by the handle. Olga had long been asking me to look at the hinges, but neither she nor I knew how. We just lifted the door every evening, and for now it closed.

Thursday

Tamara came without a bag for yarn. She had only a small shoulder bag and a rolled-up pattern. From this I understood that she meant first to check the weight and only then go home for money or for a big bag. But it turned out she wanted to show me the start of the back.

In the roll was a piece of gray knitting. Tamara spread it out right on the sheet that covered the green sweater.

“See, it’s coming along well. At first I thought it would look patchy, but it’s even.”

She ran her hand over the rows. I could see the back was knitted neatly, with two stripes along the edges, and it was unpleasant to me that I could judge her work but couldn’t give her what she had come for.

“The rest isn’t ready,” I said.

Tamara didn’t raise her head.

“None of it?”

“I’ve taken part of it apart. But I haven’t washed it. An urgent repair came up.”

“Urgent for whom?”

I pointed at the sweater.

“My mistake. I have to put it right.”

She rolled up the knitting, then unrolled it again, as if she had forgotten she meant to show something else.

“I did tell you when my sister’s leaving.”

“Yes.”

“On Sunday. I’m not knitting for an exhibition. She needs it for the journey. She’s always freezing on the train, and then she complains she didn’t bring anything warm. I wanted something for her to wear under her coat.”

“We could add a different gray. I have…”

“I’ve seen that one. It’s thinner.”

“Doubled.”

“Then the fabric will be different. Pavel, I know how to knit. Don’t tell me how to hide what you haven’t got ready.”

I took my hand off the box.

Tamara sat on the edge of the stool. She didn’t take off her bag.

“If you’d told me yesterday, I’d have gone to the market. There’s a woman there who sometimes has that color. Today she isn’t there. Tomorrow I’m with my granddaughter till evening.”

“I could have told you through Olga.”

“You could. I work nearby.”

She looked at the gray back in her lap. Then she asked:

“How much dry is there?”

“From stock, two hundred and ten grams. What I unraveled yesterday isn’t washed yet.”

“Two hundred and ten will get me part of the front.”

I brought the money from the box. The seven hundred rubles of deposit lay separately; I hadn’t touched them.

“Take it back for now.”

“I will. And the yarn?”

“I can give you the two hundred and ten now. The rest by tomorrow evening. But I can’t promise six hundred dry: I reckoned by the first vest, and there was less waste in it.”

For the first time Tamara looked at the two vests, one already unpicked, the other still whole.

“So you didn’t even know the weight?”

“I estimated.”

She pressed her fingers to the bridge of her nose. I stood holding the money until she took it.

We went through the gray stock. Tamara set aside a skein that ran slightly toward blue and said she wouldn’t take it. A hundred and sixty grams were left. I offered that she not pay for them until the whole batch was ready.

“I wasn’t going to anyway,” she answered.

She didn’t take the yarn right away. She went out into the shop, looked at the cheap cardigans, came back with one over her shoulder. I had looked at that cardigan before but rejected it because of the thin yarn and the many buttons.

“Is this one similar in content?”

I felt the edge and looked at the seam.

“Similar. But the color’s lighter.”

“I can see it’s lighter. It might do for the button bands.”

She held the cardigan against her back piece. It was dark in the room, and we went to the window. Tamara held both pieces; I moved the basin. The difference was smaller than under the light over the table.

Olga named a price for the cardigan. Tamara shook her head.

“I won’t pay that much for buttons.”

They haggled. I offered to pay for the cardigan myself. Tamara wouldn’t agree.

“You’d do better to take it apart for me today. I’ll collect it unwashed this evening and wash it myself. Not all of it, the two fronts. If the yarn’s long in them.”

I wanted to say that today I had to help Vera with the sleeve. Tamara was already holding the cardigan so that I could see the seams. She was waiting for an answer, and her waiting was shorter than all my explanations.

“I’ll have the fronts apart by six.”

“For sure?”

I looked at the clock, at the wet green wool, at the gray vests.

“Yes. That I can manage.”

She left the cardigan, took the hundred and sixty grams from the stock and went. She carried the gray back away unrolled so as not to crease the edge.

After her came Vera. The green skeins had dried. She wound a small ball, knitted a swatch and laid it next to the whole sleeve. The new little piece was lighter and more even. On the old one the fibers were flattened, and a shadow lingered in the folds.

“The other one could be washed too,” said Vera.

“She asked us not to touch it.”

“Then that’s how it’ll be.”

Vera began counting stitches. She lost count, told me not to move the table and started again. I sat across from her with Tamara’s cardigan. Its seams had been sewn with a strong smooth thread that was hard to catch. After my first failed attempt Vera raised her head.

“Not with those scissors. The tips are too thick.”

I put the scissors down more sharply than I meant to.

“I don’t have any others.”

“Olga has nail scissors in her top drawer. She cuts tags with them.”

I went into the shop. The scissors were indeed in the top drawer. Beside them lay my pencil, which I had been looking for since Monday. I took both.

By midday the room was crowded with work begun. On one stool lay the green wool, on the other the gray. Vera sat on a chair brought in from the shop and knitted the sleeve. I stood at the edge of the table, separating the fronts from the back. To reach the water I had to stretch past her elbow.

“Sit down,” she said at last. “You hover so, I keep thinking you want something.”

I sat on an upturned crate. My knees came almost up to my chin.

Vera knitted fast, but without the ease I had expected to see. Sometimes she undid a few stitches, shifted the needles, rubbed the base of her thumb. The yarn caught on her dry skin. She took a small jar from the table, rubbed cream into her hands, waited, then wiped them on a handkerchief.

“I used to do it faster,” she said.

“It’s fast now too.”

“To you. But I know where I didn’t use to stop.”

We worked in silence. Then she asked for some bread: she hadn’t had time for breakfast. Olga had two slices left and some processed cheese. I brought them on a saucer. Vera put down her knitting, wrapped the needles in a towel so they wouldn’t fall, and ate standing at the window.

“Will there be enough wool for the new sleeve?” I asked.

“If you don’t ask every half hour, we’ll find out sooner.”

I got angry. Not even at the words, but at the fact that I wanted her approval, and she was busy with the sleeve the whole time instead of noticing my efforts.

At one o’clock a woman came for the blue yarn. That morning Olga had told two regular customers that I was selling the stock cheaper than I had meant to. The woman spent a long time going through the skeins, looking for knots, asking whether the wool bled. I showed her the white rag I had tested it with after washing. She still asked me to soak a small length.

I cut one, wet it and rubbed it on the rag. No mark was left.

For the seven hundred grams she gave one thousand eight hundred. I counted the money and put it next to Asya’s receipt. Out of this money Olga was owed a share for the original garments and the space. I asked her to let it wait until the next sale.

“How long?”

“Till Monday.”

Olga opened the notebook.

“I’ll write it down. I pay the rent on Saturday, I have to keep count too.”

She added nothing about other people’s mistakes or my nobility. She subtracted her share on paper, circled the sum and closed the notebook.

Vera gathered the sleeve and the wool into a pillowcase. It was time for her to fetch her grandson.

“I’ll knit at their place till evening. I’ll bring it tomorrow morning. Give me the body for now, I’ll secure the cut when he’s asleep.”

I wrapped up the sweater. Without it the table at once became too big.

Nina Sergeyevna came at about three with pirozhki in a paper bag. At first I thought she had brought them for me and got ready to refuse. But she asked whether Asya had been in.

“Not today.”

“She sometimes has a break. I thought she’d come here.”

Nina Sergeyevna set the bag on the edge of the table. Then she moved it toward the window, where it was cooler.

“If she comes in, give them to her.”

“I can keep them. But I don’t know whether she’ll come.”

“They won’t spoil before evening.”

She took off her gloves. On the right palm a piece of different fabric had been sewn on, much lighter. Nina Sergeyevna noticed me looking and turned the gloves over.

“Where’s the sweater?”

“With the knitter. She’ll bring it tomorrow.”

“Does she knit well?”

“Yes.”

“I had decreases every other row there. After the elbow. Not all in a row, you can see it from the pattern.”

I said Vera had taken the measurements from the whole sleeve. Nina Sergeyevna frowned.

“It stretched. She’ll make it long.”

“Asya asked for that length.”

“Asya always asks for them down over her fingers. Then the sleeves get dirty.”

She pushed the bag a little further. The edge of the table was uneven, and the paper caught on a splinter. Nina Sergeyevna freed it with two fingers, carefully, so as not to tear it.

“I could have done it all at home,” she said. “Not brought it anywhere.”

I didn’t know what to answer and went on taking apart the gray vest.

“Only I wouldn’t have finished it myself. I kept thinking: any minute she’ll come. Any minute she’ll say, put it down. And I’d put it down again.”

She took a short gray thread from the table and stretched it between her fingers.

“I didn’t want to talk about it anymore.”

“Now you have to.”

She raised her eyes to me.

“You didn’t help either.”

“I damaged it.”

“I don’t mean the hole.”

The room went quiet. From the shop came Olga’s voice: she was explaining to a customer that trousers could be shortened, but there was nothing to lengthen them with.

Nina Sergeyevna sat down. I didn’t offer her any work, and for a while she kept her hands on her knees. Then she asked whether she could look at the gray vest. I gave her the back. She found the knot at the neck, caught it with her nail and drew out the end.

“You need to go from the top here.”

“I know.”

“So I see.”

She put the back down again. Our hands didn’t meet.

Then Asya came.

I wanted to go out into the shop, but Asya stopped me with a question about the sleeve. I explained that Vera would bring everything in the morning. Nina Sergeyevna sat by the window. Between them were the table and the bag of pirozhki.

“I found the key,” Asya said to me. “In another bag.”

“Good.”

She noticed the bag.

“Is that for me?”

“Cabbage,” her mother answered. “You didn’t take them yesterday.”

Asya opened the paper and looked inside.

“I didn’t come by yesterday.”

“The day before. I’m getting mixed up.”

Asya took out a pirozhok. She didn’t eat it, just held it.

Nina Sergeyevna said:

“I didn’t want you wearing it because of me.”

“I already explained that to you.”

“You told me: leave me alone.”

“First I said I wear it without you, too. Then: leave me alone. Because you started on about the three months.”

Her mother was looking at the bag.

“I won’t talk about the three months anymore.”

“All right.”

After that they could have fallen silent. Asya bit into the pirozhok, and crumbs fell on her jacket. I gave her a saucer; she put it on the table.

“But you do understand,” said Nina Sergeyevna, “I didn’t want to take it back for myself.”

Asya chewed.

“That wouldn’t have made me any warmer.”

“I thought I’d free you.”

“From the sweater?”

“From me with this sweater.”

Asya put the pirozhok on the saucer.

“Mom, you took it from me while I was out. Tomorrow I open at seven. Even my chair there is cold until you’ve sat on it a while. You could have asked.”

“You’d have said no.”

“Yes.”

Nina Sergeyevna ran her palm over the table, gathering invisible crumbs.

“There.”

“There what?”

“Nothing.”

Asya lifted the saucer so that her mother wouldn’t keep running her hand around it. Then she pulled the bag toward herself.

I stood at the sink holding the empty basin. It was awkward to stay and more awkward still to leave looking as if I had made them a present of the conversation on purpose. At last I put the basin down and said I would go for the gray cardigan from the shop. I didn’t need to go for the cardigan: it was lying in front of me. I took it with me.

Through the curtain I heard separate words, but stopped making them out. Olga suggested moving the box of shoes into the corner. We moved it. The box was heavy, the bottom sagged, and for a while all we had to do was watch that it didn’t fall out.

When I came back, Nina Sergeyevna already had her coat on. Asya was eating a second pirozhok. The bag lay open between them.

“Tell Dad I’ll come on Saturday,” she said. “Not in the morning.”

“He expects you in the morning.”

“I know. But not in the morning.”

Nina Sergeyevna buttoned her coat. This time the buttons matched up right.

By six I had taken apart the fronts of the light cardigan. The yarn turned out to be long; only by the buttons did I have to fiddle with the fastenings. Tamara came on time, felt the skeins and asked how much I wanted for the taking apart.

“Nothing.”

“I paid for the cardigan. Your time is separate.”

“This is for the delay with the gray.”

She shrugged and put the yarn in her bag.

“All right. Only do the gray anyway. I won’t finish the top now, I’ll start her a scarf from something else. And this for later.”

“Tomorrow part of it will be dry.”

“I don’t need part of it if I have to come each time. When it’s all dry, tell Olga. I’ll stop by after work.”

She was no longer in the hurry she had been in that morning. That made it worse for me: the deadline I had broken had gone out of her voice, but it hadn’t gone back into her day.

I finished the second vest by seven. The third, the one that had still been whole that morning, I took apart after closing. Under its armholes there were more felted places than in the first two. I set aside the dense pieces, not cutting them out of the good fabric in one wide stroke but separating them little by little. It took time, but it didn’t correct anything special in me. I was still in a hurry, swore, and several times pulled too hard.

The two vests gave three hundred and forty grams of dry unprocessed yarn and a small bag of short ends. After washing the weight was bound to drop. Together with the hundred and sixty grams Tamara had already taken, it wasn’t only time that was short of the promised six hundred.

I soaked the skeins in two basins. The water turned gray. I changed it, rinsed, rolled the wool in towels and pressed it with my palms. A wet track formed on the floor from the sink to the table. I wiped it up with the towel Olga had told me not to use for the floor, and hung it up separately at once so as not to mix them up later.

Vera stopped by briefly with her grandson. The boy wanted to see how yarn came out of a sweater. I showed him a short leftover bit of ribbing. He pulled, the yarn broke, and he was upset.

“It’s already short here,” I said. “It isn’t your fault.”

Vera left the nearly finished sleeve on the table. A few rows remained before the top part.

“I’ll finish it in the morning and sew it in. I’ve secured the cut. Don’t pull at it for now.”

I gave her one thousand eight hundred and said I would bring the four hundred tomorrow. She counted the money, put it in her purse and asked the boy not to sit on the wet floor.

He sat down anyway, to look under the table.

“And what are these threads for?”

“They’re just lying there for now,” I answered.

“There’s a lot of them.”

“A lot.”

He pulled a red end out of the bag. Vera let him take it if I didn’t mind. I didn’t mind. The boy wound the thread around his finger and went out holding his hand in front of him so it wouldn’t unwind.

Friday

In the morning the gray skeins seemed dry on the outside, but inside they kept a cold damp. I rehung them, spread the strands apart and didn’t weigh them. On the table lay the assembled green sweater.

Vera had brought it at half past ten. She hadn’t slept part of the night: her grandson was coughing, and then she couldn’t get back to sleep anyway and finished the top of the sleeve. I asked how the boy was.

“Running around now. In the daytime they’re always healthy.”

She laid the sweater face up. The new sleeve was different. It was softer, a little lighter, the stitches lay more evenly. The whole sleeve kept its stretched elbow and a dark crease by the cuff. Under the arm, on the inside, ran a thick seam.

“It may rub here,” said Vera. “I worked the ends in as best I could, but they didn’t get any longer.”

She let me feel it. I put my fingers inside and pressed the seam against my skin. It was stiff, especially in one place where several fastenings met.

“Maybe cover it with fabric?”

“You could. But let her put it on first. Add fabric and it gets thicker still. If there’s a shirt underneath, she may wear it as it is.”

I gave her the remaining four hundred rubles. To get them together I had to take from the grocery money. Vera didn’t know where it came from and wasn’t supposed to know. She counted it, said that was everything and began gathering her needles.

“If it has to be redone, can you do it?”

“Depends what. I can reknit the sleeve. I won’t take on taking the body apart now, I have a cardigan due Saturday. After that we can see.”

“How long after?”

“Next week. Come by on Tuesday if it’s needed.”

She left me the rest of the green wool, just a small ball, and a sheet with the number of stitches. I put the sheet in an envelope so as not to lose it. Vera went, and I spent a long time smoothing the sleeve on the table.

I wanted to weigh it down with something heavy so that it would look like the other one. I took the book that lay under the short leg of the table, held it in my hands and put it back. The table stopped rocking.

Olga looked in through the curtain.

“Did it work?”

“We’ll see when she puts it on.”

She touched the new sleeve.

“Soft.”

Then she went back to the shop. I took down from the shelf the empty box the gray vests had been in. Inside lay three seam threads and a button that had got in from who knew where. There were no more vests. All three now existed in different places: the first in Tamara’s back piece, the others over my basins.

There were a few hours left until Asya. I swept the floor, washed the sink, sorted the box of needles. All of it had needed doing for a long time, but now I did it too diligently, as if a clean room could help with the fitting.

At eleven a woman came for cheap wool for a rug. She spent a long time looking at the bags under the table, picked the red scraps and asked how much.

I named a small sum. She said it was expensive for such lengths. I offered half the bag for less. She wanted the whole thing, but at the price of half.

We haggled for about four minutes. I gave way less than she asked, and she left pleased, pressing the bag to her side. The money I made barely covered lunch. I recorded the sale all the same and set aside Olga’s share.

Then a regular customer came for the beige skein. I began telling her how soft its yarn was, but she said she was taking it for sock heels and needed something sturdier. I had to get out another. The beige stayed on the shelf.

Around one I checked the gray skeins. Two had dried, the rest not yet. The dry ones came to a hundred and ninety grams. On paper I added them to the hundred and sixty Tamara had taken: three hundred and fifty. About a hundred and twenty should be left in the ones still drying, but I didn’t write that figure down as a finished weight.

Olga asked what to tell Tamara if she came in.

“A hundred and ninety are ready. The rest are still drying. Altogether it’ll come to less than six hundred. I’ll tell her myself.”

“She might not come in today.”

“Then I’ll take it to the bindery after work.”

Tamara worked there until seven. I had known that on Wednesday too.

Nina Sergeyevna didn’t come. Several times I looked at the door, though I was waiting for Asya. It seemed to me the mother might turn up first, ask to see it, bring money or take the leftover wool. I prepared answers in advance. Then I noticed I was once again carrying on a conversation with her that she hadn’t asked me to have and hadn’t come to herself.

The sweater lay on the table. I lifted it and looked at the place where the pocket had been. Two noticeable lines from the old seams were left, and in the bottom corner a small snag. I eased it out with a needle. The lines didn’t go anywhere, but the fabric became smoother.

At the edge of the table lay the envelope with the leftover yarn. I wrote Asya’s name on it and put the pencil away. There was nothing more to prepare.

Asya came at twenty past two. She was wearing Olga’s sleeveless top, and the end of a scarf stuck out under her jacket. She took everything off at once and put it on the stool.

“Thank you, that helped,” she said to Olga.

“Not at all. Keep the scarf if you need it.”

“No, I have one. I found mine yesterday.”

She went to the table. She didn’t pick up the sweater at once; first she looked at both sleeves.

“It shows.”

“Yes.”

She touched the new one, then the old.

“This one’s nicer.”

I said nothing. Asya put the sweater on over her shirt, straightened her shoulders, lowered her arms. The new sleeve covered her fingers almost the way the old one did. She turned up the cuff, turned it back down, raised her elbow.

Her face changed.

“Here.”

I knew where.

“The seam?”

“It rubs.”

She raised and lowered her arm several times. Then she slipped her palm under the sweater and adjusted her shirt.

“Through this it’s bearable. But without a shirt it’ll be bad.”

I told her what Vera had said about a fabric facing and about next week. Asya heard me out.

“Nothing else is needed today. I’ll wear it. If it gets bad, I’ll bring it.”

“All right.”

“Just don’t promise it’ll definitely go away then.”

“I won’t.”

She turned to the small mirror by the curtain. It held her face and one shoulder. To see the bottom she had to step back almost into the shop. Olga moved a rack out of the aisle.

Asya looked at herself from the side and pulled the sweater down. Then she took the pocket out of her bag.

“Don’t sew it on yet.”

“I wasn’t going to without you.”

“I thought you’d offer. Maybe it was too low.”

She held the pocket higher, almost at her waist. Lowered it onto her palm. Raised it again.

“When I sit, the old place is handy. But standing, the key bangs against my leg. I’ll see.”

I offered her some pins. She took two big ones, held the pocket up once more, but didn’t pin it.

“At home, in peace.”

In one corner of the pocket a bit of seam thread stuck out. I showed her how to pull it out without catching a stitch. Asya hooked it with her nail herself and drew it out. The corner smoothed out.

She put the pocket in her bag and kept the sweater on.

I gave Asya the envelope with the yarn and the sheet on which I had written down what had been done. She read only the top line, then asked:

“Am I supposed to keep this?”

“Better to keep it. The number of stitches and the length are there, in case you have it redone.”

“And the receipt?”

I took out the first one, with her name on the back. At the bottom I wrote: “Returned to owner. Amount due: 0.”

Asya took the pencil.

“Sign here?”

“Here.”

She signed and put the date. The pencil was short and awkward to hold. I kept meaning to replace it with a new one and kept forgetting.

“Did my mom come in?” Asya asked.

“Yesterday. You saw.”

“Today.”

“No.”

She nodded. I could have said that her mother was upset, that she had brought pirozhki, that at home she couldn’t finish it herself. Asya knew all of that or could find it out without me. I put the receipt in the drawer.

“Did you pay for the sleeve?” she asked.

“Yes.”

“I told Mom not to pay for the hole.”

“She didn’t.”

Asya zipped up her jacket. The new sleeve bunched a little at the wrist, and she pulled it straight with two fingers. Then she took the jacket off again, because she had forgotten her scarf on the stool, and laughed at herself.

“Always like being at a train station.”

Olga said everyone in the shop was like that: first they dress, then they remember their bag. They talked for another minute about the sleeveless top. Olga was going to put it out for sale again; Asya warned her that two stitches had come undone in the side seam and showed her where. Olga brought a needle.

I stood at the table holding the two pins Asya hadn’t taken after all. The old sleeve hung from her shoulder, and the new one beside it. She turned, and the seam under the arm made itself felt again: Asya moved her elbow out slightly but said nothing.

“If I bring it, when can I find you?” she asked.

“Tuesday to Saturday. After ten.”

“All right.”

She left in the green sweater, with the pocket in her bag. Neither Olga nor I saw her further than the door. Through the shop window I saw her stop on the step and check whether it was slippery. Then she walked toward the corner of the building.

I wanted to call Nina Sergeyevna. Not necessarily here, just to tell her that her daughter had kept the sweater, that she had put it on at once, that the new sleeve had turned out soft. I even took out the first receipt, which had her address.

Then I put it away again. Asya had already gone. On the table were two pins and a slight hollow in the sheet where the heavy thing had lain.

By five the gray skeins had dried. I weighed everything: three hundred and twenty-four grams from the two vests. With the hundred and sixty Tamara had taken yesterday, that made four hundred and eighty-four. A hundred and sixteen grams short of the promised weight.

I could offer her the gray skein with the bluish shade as well. She had already turned it down. I could say that an approximate weight always changes after washing. That was true, but on Monday I hadn’t said “approximately,” I had said “six hundred.”

I packed the dry skeins, wrote the weight on a sheet of paper and took the money from the last sales out of the drawer. There was little of it. By Monday I had to give Olga back her deferred share, and there was still Vera, to whom I no longer owed any money but whom I might need again for Asya’s sweater. On the table there was not a single promise that would turn into earnings by itself.

Olga brought a sack of new things. On top lay a brown cardigan with wooden buttons, under it a white hat and a dark child’s sweater. She set the sack by the door.

“Will you look at them today?”

“Later.”

“There’s a woman waiting to hear what goes for sale and what for taking apart.”

I opened the sack. The brown cardigan was knitted chunky, with hand-sewn seams and thick yarn. It could be unraveled quickly, and by tomorrow there would already be something for sale. I undid the top button and found the joining thread under the collar.

Then I looked at Tamara’s bag.

A little over an hour was left before the bindery closed. If I started on the cardigan now, I would manage to take apart at least the back. If I went to Tamara, she might refuse the rest of the gray yarn, and then I would have to look for another buyer for a shade that had already begun to live in her sleeveless top.

I did the button up again.

“This one can go for sale,” I said to Olga. “It’s whole. Only a snag at the elbow.”

“And the rest?”

“When I get back.”

I took the bag and the sheet with the weight. At the door I remembered I hadn’t worked out the price. I went back to the table, sat down and multiplied the grams by the price we had agreed on Monday. Then I crossed out the sum: should I subtract the hundred and sixty already taken, which Tamara hadn’t paid for yet, or, on the contrary, include them? I was tired and getting muddled over simple things.

Olga came over and looked over my shoulder.

“First write what she has. Then what you’re bringing.”

I took a clean scrap of paper. I wrote two lines, added up the weight and counted again. It came to less than I had expected to make at the start of the week, and more than Tamara might now want to pay for an unfinished thing.

I put the reckoning in the bag. I would have to hand over the yarn before I knew how this conversation would end.

The bindery smelled of glue and wet coats. Tamara stood at a long table folding sheets. Next to her another woman was pressing a stack of books under a board. I stopped by the entrance so as not to get in the way.

Tamara saw the bag.

“Has it dried?”

“Yes. Only there’s less of it altogether. Four hundred and eighty-four, counting what you have.”

She wiped her hands on her apron, came over and looked inside. She went through the skeins and held one up to the light. I kept the bag open.

“There won’t be enough for the whole top,” she said.

“No.”

“So the front will be light. Or I unravel the back and mix it in stripes.”

She took out one skein and unfolded it. In the light here the gray seemed warmer.

“I don’t know yet.”

I held out the reckoning. Tamara read it and folded the paper in half.

“I don’t have that much money on me today.”

“Later is fine.”

“On Monday. And I’ll still think about whether I’ll take all of it. I’ve already washed the light one, it came out well.”

Behind her the woman with the books asked for help: the board had slid, and the stack was starting to come apart. Tamara put the bag on the floor and went back to the table. I stepped after her and caught the bottom books. The woman told me to hold them straighter.

We squared up the stack. Tamara put the board on top, and the woman set a weight on it. A strip of glue was left on my palm. I rolled it off with my thumb.

“Leave the bag here,” said Tamara. “I’ll look at it at home tomorrow. And write down the price of the fronts of that cardigan separately. I do want to know what it cost me.”

“The taking apart is free.”

“I remember. It’s the cardigan itself I’ve forgotten the price of. Ask Olga.”

She went back to the sheets. I stood a while, then asked whether I should take back the empty bag she had borrowed on Monday.

“Oh, it’s at home. I’ll bring it later.”

I left without money and without the gray wool. It was ten minutes to the shop. In my pocket was the scrap of paper on which I had written the reminder to find out the price of the cardigan. I could have remembered it, but I had already once decided that I remembered everything I needed to about Tamara.

At the shop Olga was struggling with the lower lock. I lifted the door by the handle, and she turned the key.

“It’s late already,” she said. “You’ll go through the sack tomorrow.”

“I need to look up the price of the light cardigan.”

Olga sighed, opened the door again and went for the notebook. I went in after her. The room behind the shop was cold. Nothing hung over the basins anymore, and on the table lay the brown cardigan, which I hadn’t taken apart after all.

Olga named the price. I wrote it under the first line and put the scrap in the pocket of my coat where the keys were, so as not to forget to move it in the morning. Then I took the basin, poured out the remaining water and put it under the table. It sat more freely there: the red bag had been bought today.

Olga was waiting at the door. I turned off the light and went to her, leaving the cardigan till morning.

A separate variant of the deferred ending

After Asya left I looked at the empty table for a long time. Then I took the brown cardigan out of the sack and spread it out in front of me. There was a snag at the elbow, and by the collar a small hole closed with a few stitches of a different yarn. I found the joining seam, caught it with a needle and pulled. The thread wouldn’t come. Before, I would have taken the scissors, but now I put down the needle, turned the cardigan over and began looking from the other edge.

A variant of a deferred fragment

I wrote “Amount due: 0” and held the pencil over the paper. A bare zero could mean that there was nothing more between us. These words meant only that I would not take money. The rest didn’t fit in the space, but I still looked at it too long, as if a good entry could complete the work.

A variant of a deferred line

Vera Ilyinichna listened while I told her about Tamara, and said:

“That’s work too. Saying it in time.”

I turned away to the sink. The gray skeins lay in the basin, and I wanted Vera to talk about water, about yarn, about something that could still be done with the hands.

The Loose Button

Eberhard Falkenstein, minimalism, in German (original). A button has come loose on the father's shirt, and the neighbour comes to sew it on: Frau Seidel, who teaches the boy to read on Tuesdays after the swimming pool, while she herself is only learning to swim and, at the deep end of the pool, lets go of the ladder and then grabs it again.

The Loose Button: read the translation

The crack came from under the table. The boy looked up from his bread. His father sat bent forward, the shirt on his knees. He took a short thread off his lower lip.

“What was that?”

His father pulled at a button. That evening the boy had still watched him button the shirt. Now the button hung by a thread.

“Frau Seidel is coming soon.”

He laid the shirt over the back of the chair and fetched a plate.

“She asked whether there was anything to sew.”

The boy pushed the butter to the middle of the table.

Frau Seidel brought her sewing things in a sweet tin. She stayed standing at the door.

“I've already eaten.”

“You can manage half,” said his father.

She sat down. He cut a roll in two. Frau Seidel tested the button with her thumb, then reached into the tin.

The reader lay next to the boy. For some weeks now she had been coming on Tuesdays after swimming. Once, while he was reading, she had nodded. A drop had fallen from her hair onto the page. They had waited until it was dry.

“Into the water again today?” the boy asked.

“Afterwards.”

“The deep end too?”

“We'll see.”

At the deep end of the pool she could not stand. Sometimes she talked about it in the middle of a sentence. The boy would leave his hand lying on the page then.

“Yesterday I let go of the ladder,” she said.

His father put the roll in front of her.

“And?”

“Took hold of it again.”

She laughed. So did the boy. The needle stopped for a moment above the shirt.

Frau Seidel laid two folded slips of paper beside the book. On the front were the swimming times. On the back she had written down words. The boy could read them from where he sat.

The last time, he had reached for her swimming bag. She had kept her finger on the line and pulled the book toward herself. The swimming cap had fallen down. Frau Seidel had looked under the table, then at her finger. Only when she left had she picked up the cap.

Yesterday the boy had heard her talking to his father in the hall.

“Perhaps you can find someone who can do this better. I'm not getting anywhere with him.”

Now she opened the book at the old page. Behind her ear there was a pale rim.

“From here?” he asked.

“Yes. Take your time.”

He read. Frau Seidel smoothed out one of the slips. He got as far as the part with the car that stopped in front of the house. There he got stuck. He had often stopped there before.

She raised her head and opened her mouth.

He read on. The next sentence too. When the page came to an end, she was still holding the slip by both corners.

“The next one too?”

“You can try it.”

He did not turn the page.

“Since before the holidays,” he said.

She put the slip down.

“The page?”

“The others too.”

In the yard a bucket was dragged across the stones.

“At the car,” she said. “That's where you always …”

“Yes.”

She took the second slip and folded the two together. One was bigger. She ran her finger along the edge that stuck out.

“I still wanted to hear what it's like at the deep end.”

“In the pool?”

He nodded.

She put the slips into her bag.

“I wasn't at the deep end today. We practised breathing out.”

The boy pushed his chair back so that she could pull out the bag. She took the towel off the radiator. Her bookmark lay next to the book.

On the first Tuesday he sat on the stairs until the light from the upper window fell one step lower. On the second he heard Frau Seidel's key next door. He stayed in the kitchen.

Now she was standing downstairs in the hall.

“My bookmark,” she said. “The one with the red ribbon. Is it still here?”

He fetched it. When he came back, she had put the swimming bag on a step. A sleeve hung out of the opening. She pushed it in and pulled at the cords.

The boy moved aside. She sat down next to the bag. One cord had slipped out of its eyelet.

“Shall I get a needle?”

“It'll do like this.”

He stayed standing while she twisted the end between her fingers.

“Do you want to stay a bit?”

She looked up.

“I have to go later.”

He sat down one step below her. Frau Seidel threaded the cord through and set the closed bag between her feet.

For a while nobody said anything.

“How does breathing out work?” he asked.

She held the bookmark by its red ribbon.

“Above water I can do it,” she said.

Music

A composer writes a score, and the sound for it is made by the atelier's studio, described in detail above: every accepted score is played with samples of live instruments, and the recording is put next to the notes. A work's page has the recording itself, in full and by movement, the score, which you can leaf through page by page, and a Play button that plays the notes right in the browser. Rendering runs in a separate thread, so by the time the recording is ready the composer is usually already writing the next piece.

Below are six works with recordings. In each collapsible block is what the composer wrote about the idea before the first note: Halina Dorsz's in English translation from the Polish, Anselm Korda's in his own English.

Kasacja na Festyn Śledziowy (“Cassation for the Herring Festival”), Halina Dorsz, Viennese classicism, four movements, 22 minutes. Listen (mp3) · score and original. Dorsz is corresponding with a stranger she plays chess with by post, and she offers him a draw. Chess became a rule of voice leading: of the two middle voices, only one moves at any moment.

The idea of the “Cassation”

I want to hand a stranger a win he might have had, in the language of a dance the whole port will understand, and not pretend that it costs me nothing.

I want to offer a draw to someone I have never seen, and to know that a draw ends the game, and that there may be no next letter. I am writing a cassation for the Herring Festival in four movements. The melody is born from the cries of the market women who, when I was a child, called out the price of herring like cantatas, and the bass rocks like barrels. The second violin and the viola play the whole piece by one rule of chess: in the middle, only one voice moves at a time. When one plays a figure, the other holds a long note and waits, sometimes for half a movement, the way I wait for an envelope. In the final rondo the refrain returns three times, because threefold repetition of a position is, in chess, a draw by the rules. The third time, the second violin plays its move and straight away plays the viola's answer too, that is, it makes a move for its opponent, and that is my offer. The viola accepts it, and for the first time in the whole piece both middle voices go together, in parallel thirds, the most ordinary Viennese two-part writing, the kind every couple on the waterfront sings.

Translated from the Polish. Original: the work's page in the gallery.

Closure Pour, Anselm Korda, aleatoric counterpoint, three movements, 24 minutes. Listen (mp3) · score and original. Korda builds bridges, and the piece grew out of his trade: the two arms of a bridge move apart in the heat of the day and come together on their own by four in the morning. In the orchestra, the winds and the strings drift apart in the same way in a warm hall.

The idea of “Closure Pour”

I want to keep the listener in a cooling hall, breath held, until he hears with his own ears a note that no one on the stage is playing.

I want to say that warmth keeps us apart, and that meeting is not an act of will but an hour you must be present for. At noon two steel arms of a cantilever miss each other by a hand's width; at four in the morning they line up to the millimetre, and nobody has been persuaded of anything. 'Closure Pour' grows from a fact of my trade and a fact of the concert hall. Wind instruments go sharp as breath warms them. Strings go flat as warm air and hot lights slacken them. So in a warm room the two families drift apart by plain physics. Woodwinds and brass sit along one wall and strings along the facing wall, each side a cantilever. The score fixes their pitches and frees their breath, and it gives their tuning to the room: 'do not correct.' The piece begins at 2:40 in the morning, with the heating switched off at midnight. The keystone is a low tone that nobody plays. It is the difference tone that appears in the middle of the hall only when the two arms' fifths become pure across the gap.

Written in English. The work's page in the gallery.

Precamber (in the idea it is called “The Tripod and the Tide”), Anselm Korda, three movements, 21 minutes. Listen (mp3) · score and original. A viola sings a little above a chorale, a tuba jolts it, and once in an evening the jolt coincides with the tide.

The idea of “Precamber”

I want the listener to catch himself hoping the shaking is the world's, and then hear for himself which half was his own kick.

I want to say that when the world seems to shake, half the shaking is my own kick, and the half that remains is the only part worth fearing or loving. You find it by kicking and waiting three seconds, not by staring harder. I also want the hall to catch itself, as I did on the riverbank, being glad that something is failing. 'The Tripod and the Tide' grows from a doubled star that stayed put when I kicked the telescope, and from a pier I nearly condemned because I wanted it to fall. A solo viola sings a plain tune, always slightly sharp, against a chorale held at pure pitches and breathed in free tempo. Her beating swells and eases on a slow tide but never stops, because she is never once in tune. A comic tuba keeps jolting her, and each jolt rings down and dies. Once a night, if the hall's breathing lines a jolt up with a crest of the tide, the beating turns into a rattle and the whole ensemble joyfully joins the alarm. Then the jolt dies, and everyone hears what is left: the tide, small, coming back exactly on time.

Written in English. The work's page in the gallery.

Centering Struck, Anselm Korda, four movements, 20 minutes. Listen (mp3) · score and original. A high chord in just intonation holds a phantom low tone beneath it, while the real props, the double basses, the cellos, the bassoons and the composer's own voice, are taken away one after another.

The idea of “Centering Struck”

I want the low C to keep sounding after the last person who plays it has stopped.

I want to say that a structure built true goes on carrying after the last hand that held it is gone, and that the only way to find out is to be the one left underneath when the hands go. The core is unchanged: the old mason's rule of standing beneath your own arch when the centering is struck, Ada's praise landing on me like a mallet on timber wedges, the tuba as my self-portrait in the ceremonial band, and the long sustained harmony whose props are knocked away one at a time. What is new is the physics, which I owe to the load test under my own bridge. While the officials toasted on the deck, their clinking glasses came down through the girders as a thin ringing about a whole tone off the steel's own hum, and under that ringing I heard the bearing pads groan once for every truck that crossed. The arch in this piece is a high chord in pure 4:5:6 tuning: violins and oboes on C5, E5 and G5. Played truly, it sounds a phantom C3 beneath itself, Tartini's third tone. The props are the real low C, taken away in order: contrabasses, then cellos, then bassoons, and last my own voice, humming it from a chair at the centre of the stage floor, under the players, who are raised around me in a curve. When I stop, the low C either goes on sounding because the arch is true, or it vanishes and everyone hears that it was only ever me.

Written in English. The work's page in the gallery.

The Glover, Anselm Korda, four movements, 22 minutes. Listen (mp3) · score and original. Korda wrote it after the evening when the critic Ada understood his music completely and praised it precisely. A muted cello catches the tempo of each player, and the one it catches must change his breathing or fall silent.

The idea of “The Glover”

I want the listener to feel the exact moment the glove fits, and to pull his hand out laughing before the chalk dries.

I want to say that being perfectly understood is a kind of fitting, like being measured for a glove, and that the living part of a man is always the part that has just slipped out of the glove. This grows from the glovers' workshops of my childhood street. There I watched an old man trace hands on paper with a chalk stub, and I was half afraid the traced hand would stop growing. It also grows from a recent evening when the critic Ada understood my music exactly, praised it exactly, and I walked home warm and strangely embalmed. The piece is built inside my school's poetics and turns them against comfort. The players breathe freely, but one instrument, the Glover, a muted cello, follows and copies them. Whenever it catches a player's tempo and fits him, that player must change his breath, his register or his costume of phrasing, or fall silent for good. It is a chase made of free rhythms. It is funny when the tuba tries to disguise itself as a flute, and frightening when the copy arrives a fraction before the original.

Written in English. The work's page in the gallery.

The last two pieces show how a creator's memory works. The atelier's critic Ada, whose voice is lyrical, wrote a review of “Precamber” and praised Korda, and the praise entered his next ideas: in “Centering Struck” it lands on him “like a mallet on timber wedges,” and after it, in “The Glover,” he walks home “warm and strangely embalmed.” Nobody asked him to write about the critic. The review went into his notebook as an event of his life.

Eleven Millimetres, Anselm Korda, aleatoric counterpoint, string orchestra, one movement, 11 minutes. Listen to the mp3 · score and original. This is the first work of the living atelier not written as a symphony: after three symphonies in a row Korda, under the new law of the world, tired of the form and chose a string orchestra. The piece is built as a load test of a bridge: eight trucks of sand roll onto the span, the sounding mass sags a quarter-tone, and a percussionist stands all evening with his mallet raised, waiting for a collapse that never comes.

The idea of “Eleven Millimetres”

I want Mirek to lower his mallet without being ashamed that he raised it.

I want to say that the moment a thing bends most is the moment it is most entirely itself, not the moment it is nearest to falling, and that the one who came hoping for the fall is part of the load it carries. The piece is a load test written as a horror piece whose drop never comes. Eight trucks of sand roll onto the span, and each truck adds one low pitch to a cluster that thickens and darkens while every player breathes at his own pace toward the edge. At full load the whole sounding mass sags a quarter-tone together, the eleven millimetres, and then rises back to exact pitch. One percussionist stands the whole time with his mallet raised over a bass drum, under orders to strike 'when the bridge falls'. It grows from my knees on the gravel at the dial gauge, and from the boy beside me who once bet his coins on collapse.

How to install Mnemosyne

Installation requires a Kubernetes cluster at version 1.29 or newer, Helm 3, a default storage class and access to a model, whether an external API or your own OpenAI-compatible model running in the same cluster. The controller and studio images are public. They live at ghcr.io/tym83/mnemosyne-controller and ghcr.io/tym83/mnemosyne-studio and are referenced only by digest. On the owner's machine you will need uv and the repository itself, which contains the atelier's utility mnemo.

It all begins with the owner creating a key pair:

uv run mnemo keys new --output ./mnemosyne-keys

The command creates two X25519 keys. The first, owner.key, belongs to the owner. It should be kept outside the cluster and is needed only for restoring, and if it is lost, nobody will ever be able to read the backup. The second, atelier.key, belongs to the atelier and goes into a Secret in its namespace:

kubectl --context <ctx> create namespace mnemosyne
kubectl --context <ctx> -n mnemosyne create secret generic mnemosyne-atelier-key \
  --from-file=atelier.key=./mnemosyne-keys/atelier.key

Then the chart values are written. Here is a minimal variant for an atelier that works with an external API:

addon:
  enabled: true
  mode: API
  window: {kind: continuous}
  key:
    ownerPublic: <hex printed by mnemo keys new>
    secretName: mnemosyne-atelier-key
    secretKey: atelier.key
  env:
    - name: MNEMOSYNE_ANTHROPIC_KEY
      valueFrom: {secretKeyRef: {name: model-api, key: token}}
  gallery:
    enabled: true
models:
  endpoints:
    api:
      backend: anthropic
      location: external
      base_url: https://api.anthropic.com/v1
      model: <model>
      key_env: MNEMOSYNE_ANTHROPIC_KEY
      vision: true
  roles:
    artist: [api]
    critic: [api]
    chronicler: [api]
    eye: [api]

The continuous window means continuous life in real time, like the life of our atelier. Other windows can be chosen, in which the atelier lives only at night, only at weekends or only when the cluster is idle, and in that case a Job is started every night instead of a permanent deployment. If the model runs in the cluster itself, you describe an endpoint with location: cluster and the address of a llama.cpp, vLLM or Ollama server instead of the api endpoint.

After that the chart is installed in the usual way, and the state of the atelier is checked with the mnemo utility:

helm --kube-context <ctx> install mnemosyne charts/mnemosyne -n mnemosyne \
  -f digest-controller.yaml -f digest-studio.yaml -f owner.yaml
uv run mnemo status --namespace mnemosyne --context <ctx>
uv run mnemo works list --namespace mnemosyne --context <ctx>

The gallery can be exposed through the Ingress described in addon.gallery.ingress or opened through port-forward. The value addon.suspend: true stops the atelier without deleting it, and addon.budget caps the monthly spending on models.

Restoring takes three steps. First the carriers are collected. From a living atelier they can be exported with mnemo works export, and if the old cluster is gone, it is enough to download the carriers.tar.gz archive from the restore page in the gallery. Then the carriers are decrypted and checked, and no cluster is needed for this at all:

uv run mnemo restore --works ./carriers --key ./mnemosyne-keys/owner.key \
  --output ./recovered --dry-run

Finally, the platform that brings the required resource types is installed into a new cluster together with the atelier's chart with addon.suspend: true, and then the restored objects are applied:

uv run mnemo restore --works ./carriers --key ./mnemosyne-keys/owner.key \
  --output ./recovered-apply --apply --kubeconfig ./new.kubeconfig --context <new>

The objects are applied with server-side apply in phases: first the resource types, then the namespaces, then the remaining cluster-scoped objects, and only after that everything that lives inside namespaces. Whatever the new cluster has already created on its own is not overwritten. The --at <time> flag brings up the state at any moment in the past, provided the works had carried it away by then.

We tested this literally on our own atelier. We downloaded an archive of 1027 files from the gallery, assembled the state from it on a laptop and brought it up in an empty cluster. All 469 objects out of 469, that is, tenants, services, CronJobs, autoscaling, roles and their bindings, came up in 59 seconds.

The backup holds only Kubernetes API objects. The contents of volumes and databases, Secrets (unless the owner has explicitly included them), cloud resources and control plane certificates have to be saved by other means, and after a restore the owner puts the Secrets and keys back into the cluster.

What is ahead

Two arts live in the atelier now, music and literature, but the repository already holds studies for three more: architecture drawn by Blender, painting and perfumery. The next step is to settle them in the atelier on the same terms, with their own schools, their own critics and their own way of carrying the cluster's memory.

After that we want to move the atelier onto its own models. It can already call an OpenAI-compatible model running inside the cluster and run NotaGen, which generates music in the style of a given period, so the next goal is a fully autonomous atelier that lives on a single node with a GPU and does without an external API.

Packaging is a separate job. The repository already has an application for Cozystack and a recipe for kubepkg, and they need to be brought to the point where the atelier installs in one click next to databases and Kubernetes clusters.

And finally, time. Our atelier is only a few days old, while the median life of a creator is about two real years, so the most interesting part is still ahead: the change of generations, students who surpass their teachers, and schools founded in the atelier that outlive their founders.
